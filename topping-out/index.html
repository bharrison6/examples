Make a call

Before you start. A call is a judgment made before the work, whose consequence lands somewhere else. Guidehow to use this demo

You are the general contractor and the clock is the contract. What was that decision worth on the Monday you made it?

Where this sits: Demo 10 of 16, Part four. Part four applies the same habit — predict, run it, let the result correct you — inside a real discipline. This is the scheduling case, and it needs network diagrams, not any of the AI demos.

  1. Read the schedule. Everyone enters the same seed code, so the weather and every roll are identical for every team. Press Start the job, then read the float column before deciding anything.
  2. Run a week. Predict which activity will be critical, then Commit the week. No undo, ever.
  3. Make a call. Answer what is on the table. Not answering is answering — every call defaults to its free, most exposed option.
  4. Debrief. Grade the decision, not the outcome.

Three ways to look at the same job

4D cutaway — a section through the building driven by real progress. The finished job is drawn as a faint dashed ghost and work fills it in; crews stand on the floor they are working, so a stacked floor looks crowded and turns red. Gantt — bars from early start to early finish, a thin tail out to late finish for float, critical work in red, cure and inspection holds hatched, today and the contract date both marked. Network — the precedence diagram, with relationship type and lag on every arrow and the driving path thick and red. It is computed every week, never declared, so it moves and the game says so when it does.

Three things that cost teams the game

  • Float is shared, not spare. Spend it on one activity and the ones behind it no longer have it.
  • Not answering is answering. The default is always the most exposed option.
  • Cure and inspection holds cannot be compressed. No crew count, no overtime, no fee. Ever.

Live the schedule, the float column and the critical path

A precedence-diagram CPM is solved from src/data.js every turn. Nothing is marked critical in the data file; criticality falls out of the arithmetic below.

Illustrativea plausible small-commercial pro forma, not a real project’s numbers the contract, the money and the durations

Racer Commons is invented: 38 activities, a $4,450,000 contract, 134 working days. The figures are chosen so that schedule rather than cost-cutting dominates the score.

How it works

Forward: ES = max over predecessors (FS: EF+lag · SS: ES+lag · FF: EF+lag−duration)  |  EF = ES + duration
Backward: LF = min over successors (FS: LS−lag · SS: LS−lag+duration · FF: LF−lag)  |  LS = LF − duration
Total float = LS − ES   (shared along the chain)
Free float = earliest successor ES − your EF   (yours alone; always ≤ total float)

Walk the network in topological order for the forward pass and in reverse for the backward pass. The largest early finish is the project duration. Total float and free float are different quantities, and students collapse them into “spare time” at their own cost: at baseline MEP rough-in on Level 1 carries 9 days of total float and 1 of free float.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Float is measured to the project’s own computed finish, so it is never negative. A job twelve days behind shows no −12; performance against the contract is reported separately in calendar days. Students who have seen negative float in P6 need this said out loud.
  • Days are numbered from 0 and finish is exclusive, so EF = ES + duration. Textbook tables numbering days 1–n read one higher on EF and LF.
  • Cure and inspection holds are modelled as activities rather than lags, so you can see them sitting on the critical path.
  • Durations are crew-days of work, not fixed calendar durations: they shorten with crews and stretch when a trade is starved.

Sources

  • The CPM itself is standard precedence-diagramming method, not a cited result. The engine is checked against a hand calculation instead — all sixty numbers of the ten-activity tutorial network, printed in the CPM appendix §1.
  • Appendix §8 is the printable vocabulary; the hover glossary in the game is the authoritative copy.
Boundary: this is a teaching schedule, not a scheduling tool. It does no resource levelling, no cost loading, no per-activity calendars and no holiday calendars, and it will not tell you anything about a real project.

Live the week’s progress and the re-solved critical path

Committing a week advances five working days, then re-solves the network: completed activities pinned to the dates they actually happened, work in progress anchored to its real start with remaining duration recomputed from the crews now on it, and nothing not yet started able to begin before today.

Illustrativethe shape is the classic crash curve; the three steps are chosen to show it the crashing premium

Marginal crew output runs 1.00, 0.80, 0.65, so two crews deliver 1.80 crew-days a day, not 2.00 — about 44% off the duration for 100% more labour. Always available, never free, worth it only on the driving chain.

Illustrative25% per crew over, floored at 35% — a teachable shape, not a measured loss trade stacking

Past a zone’s cap, everyone in it loses 25% per crew over. Four crews in a two-crew zone deliver 2.0 crew-days a day — what two deliver, for twice the money.

How it works

50 hr: 1.25× hours, 2 weeks grace, then ×0.90 per week, floor 0.75 — cost 1.375× from week 1
60 hr: 1.50× hours, 1 week grace, then ×0.85 per week, floor 0.62 — cost 1.750× from week 1

Both halves of this, please. Overtime genuinely accelerates at first: 1.25× and 1.50× in week one are real, and a two-week burst on a driving activity is good play. But the premium never decays and the efficiency does — by week 5 a 50-hour crew produces 0.938 of a straight-time week at 1.375× the cost, and a sustained 60-hour crew settles at 0.930 at 1.75×. And neither touches a hold.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • One week back at straight time resets the streak completely. Real recovery is gradual and the literature does not support instant reset. Students will find this and alternate weeks to farm the grace period.
  • The 0.62 floor for sustained 60-hour weeks is an authored choice, not a quoted figure — see the Reasoned note in the appendix. Taking the literature’s 66% literally would make sixty-hour weeks better than fifty-hour weeks, which is the wrong lesson.
  • Adding a crew takes two weeks’ notice, and the mobilization charge lands when you order, not when they arrive. Releasing one is immediate and free.
  • Fatigue, absenteeism, turnover and the quality rework that sustained overtime causes are not modelled at all — only the productivity number is.

Sources

  • Business Roundtable, Report C-2, Scheduled Overtime Effect on Construction Projects (Nov 1980) — TRB catalogue record, checked 2026-09-16. Operations above 60 hours weekly for over two months “reduce productivity and increase labor costs without meaningful schedule benefits”.
  • CII SD-98 (Aug 1994) — Thomas & Raynar, later ASCE JCEM 123(2) 1997, checked 2026-09-16: “Work weeks of 5 and 6 days show losses of efficiency of 12 and 14 percent”, from 121 weeks across four projects; and “about 25 percent of the time efficiency is not impaired for durations of 2 to 4 weeks” — which is where the grace period comes from.
  • Appendix §2 has the full week-by-week table, the MCAA Bulletin OT1 comparison, and what each source could and could not be verified for.
Boundary: the productivity numbers are a teachable shape calibrated to agree with the literature’s direction, not a prediction of any real crew’s output. Do not take a multiplier off this screen into an estimate.

Live the roll each call is judged against

This is the mechanism the whole fairness claim rests on, and until this edition it was documented only in the instructor guide — a learner could not see that the rolls were seeded. The roll is a hash of the seed and the call’s identifier, and nothing else: not the week you answered, not the order you played, not anything any team did.

Scripteda hand-authored deck of nine calls with their option prices the calls and the event deck

Every call, its options, its prices and its exposures are written by hand in src/data.js. The deck is fixed; what varies between teams is only which options they buy.

Illustrativechosen so the arithmetic is teachable at the board the exposure percentages

45%, 40%, 14%, 3% and the rest are picked so that buying exposure down is sometimes right and sometimes waste, and so a first-year can price it in their head.

How it works

roll = hash(seed, callId)  →  the consequence fires when roll < the exposure of the option you bought
Weather damage = min(0.62, 0.16 × rain days)

Two teams both looking at 0.31: the one that bought the exposure down to 3% is covered, the one that left it at 45% is not. What separates them is a decision made in week 1, not a die thrown in week 9. Two engine self-tests hold this — identical rolls across two games on a seed, and rolls unchanged after a whole game played differently.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The ⚠ UNSAFE backfill option is not presented as a trade-off, and that is deliberate. Every other call is a legitimate judgment with defensible answers on both sides. This one is not: an unbraced, uncured foundation wall with fill on one side only is loaded in a direction nobody designed for, and when one lets go it rotates in on whoever is standing in the excavation. It is an OSHA excavation and structural-stability violation before it is a schedule decision. It stays in the deck because schedule pressure producing an unsafe shortcut is not hypothetical in this industry, and it is cheaper to meet in a classroom. Presenting it as a balanced option would teach that the cost is dollars and days, when the cost is a person.
  • Exposure is a stated probability against a seeded roll, not a model of real construction risk.
  • Expediting always buys back some of a delay and never all of it — recovery is capped one day short of the delay at every difficulty. It is not an undo button.
  • Rain days stop only weather-flagged work: earthwork, concrete placement, roofing and envelope. Steel erection carries no flag, so the wind-advisory card leaves the crane alone — a boundary drawn for teachability, and real erectors do stand down in 40 mph gusts.

Sources

  • The calls are authored scenarios, not cited cases. What is checked is internal: engine self-tests assert that no expedite buys back more days than the delay cost, that every call explains its jargon in plain words, and that unsafe options are rare, flagged and never the only way through.
  • Appendix §4 prints the deck, the two-by-two that grades a call, and the procurement pre-buys.
Boundary: the demo does not claim these probabilities are real construction risk rates. It claims only that deciding under a stated exposure, and being graded on the decision rather than the roll, is the habit worth building.

Live your profit, your cash curve and every call’s verdict

Score is profit: contract plus change orders, less buyout, labour, general conditions, event and call costs, expedite fees and hiring, less liquidated damages, plus bonus. The calls table and the cash S-curve are computed from this run’s actual decisions.

Measuredfrom the engine’s own CPM, 2026-09-16, one crew per activity, no overtime the do-nothing baseline

Unimpeded this network runs 89 working days against the 134-day contract — 45 working days, 63 calendar days, of cushion. Across eight measured seeds, absorbing everything averages about $185,000 and goes negative on two; playing judgment averages about $351,000.

Reasoned the four verdicts

Covered, unlucky, got away with it and went against you are the authors’ grading scheme, derived from the two things the page already knows: what exposure you carried, and how the roll landed. They are a judgement about how to grade a decision, not a measurement.

How it works

Profit = contract + change orders − buyout − labour − GC − event/call/expedite/hiring − LDs + bonus
A week late = $123,200  |  a week early = $36,400  →  a 3.4:1 asymmetry

The verdicts are a two-by-two of did you buy the exposure down against did the roll go your way, and the diagonal is the lesson. A good decision can have a bad outcome and a bad decision can have a good one.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Liquidated damages at $14,000 a calendar day on a $4.45M job are steeper than most real contracts this size, where LDs sit nearer actual carrying cost. They are set there so schedule rather than cost-cutting dominates the score. Ask the class what a $2,000-a-day LD would produce instead.
  • The cash model is a simplification: monthly pay applications, ~30-day payment, 5% retainage, and a line of credit at an illustrative ~9.4% APR. Profitable and solvent are not the same thing, which is the point of carrying it.
  • Score is profit alone. Safety, quality and the client relationship are not in the number — which is exactly why the unsafe-call block prints above the profit.
  • The leaderboard is local to this machine and survives a reload and a Reset.

Sources

  • The money model is an authored pro forma, labelled Illustrative on the stage 1 drawer. Nothing in it is a cited figure.
  • Appendix §3 and §6 carry the money table, the measured seeds and all eleven debrief questions.
Boundary: a high score here is evidence that a team read float and priced its calls on this seed. It is not evidence they would run a real job well, and it is not a claim that profit is the right way to grade construction management.

5 Stage by stage

Stage 1 — Read the schedule

Question. Where is this job's float, and which chain has none?
Predict prompt. Before they start the job: which chain has zero float? The prediction is captured on screen and echoed beside the computed answer.
Activity. Seed and difficulty in, Start the job, then read the Gantt and Network views before committing anything. 4–5 minutes.
Expected observation. Unimpeded, the network runs 89 working days against a 134-day contract — 45 working days, 63 calendar days, of cushion. One chain carries zero total float and everything else has some.
Answer key. The float column, not the bar length, identifies the critical chain. A bar can be long and have float; a two-day cure can be short and have none.
Teaching limit. Float here is measured to the project's own computed finish, so it is never negative. Students who have seen negative float in P6 need that said explicitly.

Stage 2 — Run a week

Question. What does one week actually change?
Predict prompt. Which activity is critical after this week? — asked before Commit and captured, because the engine re-solves the network afterwards and will answer it. This is the demo's signature prediction; make the room commit out loud too.
Activity. Read THE NEWS, allocate crews, Commit the week, watch five working days run in 4D. 2 minutes for the first one, seconds thereafter.
Expected observation. The prediction appears beside the computed result. Sooner or later they disagree, and the game announces the move and names the activity it moved to.
Answer key. Completed work is pinned to the dates it actually happened, work in progress is anchored to its real start, and nothing not yet started can begin before today. Criticality falls out of that.
Teaching limit. One turn is one week of five working days; the engine has no clock, no network call and no unseeded randomness.

Stage 3 — Make a call

Question. What are you buying when you answer a call?
Predict prompt. Before choosing: what do you think the free option costs you, in dollars?
Activity. Answer the call on the table — each option shows its price today and its exposure, the probability the deferred consequence fires — then Commit. Nine calls in Racer Commons, two in the tutorial.
Expected observation. Nothing is hidden: the card prints the exposure as a percentage and names the activity where the consequence will land. Buying exposure down is buying a smaller bar for the same roll.
Answer key. Every call defaults to its first option, which is always free and always the most exposed. Clicking Commit through the job takes the 45%, the 40% and all the rest without deciding to.
Teaching limit. Exposure is a stated probability against a seeded roll, not a model of real construction risk. One option is deliberately ⚠ UNSAFE and is not a trade-off — see the box below.

Stage 4 — Debrief

Question. Was that a good decision, or a good outcome?
Predict prompt. Before opening it: will your calls table read mostly “covered” or mostly “got away with it”?
Activity. Open the Debrief. One row per call: Week / Call / You chose / Cost / Exposure / The roll / Outcome. 12 minutes with the room.
Expected observation. The verdicts are a two-by-two, and the diagonal is the lesson: got away with it is where the luckiest team lives, and unlucky: you covered it and it still bit is where a team that did everything right can be sitting.
Answer key. Same seed, same roll. Put a team that left a call at 45% beside a team that bought it down to 3% and price both on the Monday they decided.
Teaching limit. Profit is the score, but the cash curve is the second set of books — a team can project a healthy profit while sitting $1,000,000 into the bank's money.

The one call that is not a trade-off

Around week six, backfilling against green unbraced foundation walls is offered three ways. Waiting costs three days; engineered kickers buy them back for $6,400; and backfilling high against one side now is free, saves the three days and is marked ⚠ UNSAFE, with the reason printed before anybody clicks. An unbraced, uncured wall loaded on one side only is an OSHA excavation and structural-stability violation before it is a schedule decision. It is in the deck deliberately, and if a team takes it the debrief opens with a red block above their profit — whether or not the wall stood. If the roll went their way it says so: and it held. That is the most dangerous possible outcome, because it teaches you it was fine. Read the block, then the number, then ask whether they would sign that decision with their own name on the citation.

6 Five misconceptions, and the moment that exposes each

These are the on-screen refutation bank too: each stage's Check yourself card offers the flawed model as a real option and names why it is wrong. State the misconception before the correction — a correction alone does not displace a model the student already holds.

(a) “Float is free time you can spend casually.” — Stage 1 Total float belongs to the whole chain and is consumed by whoever spends it first. MEP1 carries 9 days of total float but only 1 day of free float: slip it three days and the finish has not moved, but Level 2 rough-in cannot start when it wanted to. Then the switchgear card takes two days and the failed rough-in inspection takes three, on a branch that had four.
(b) “Overtime always accelerates.” — Stage 2 It genuinely does at first — a 50-hour week delivers 1.25× and a 60-hour week 1.50× in week one, and that is real acceleration, not a concession. But the cost premium applies in full from week one and never decays, while efficiency does: by week 5 a 50-hour crew produces 0.938 of a straight-time week at 1.375× the labour cost. Overtime is a burst tool for a defined push. And it does nothing at all to a cure or a hold.
(c) “The critical path is fixed at baseline.” — Stage 2 It is recomputed every Monday. ROOF sits off the path at baseline with six days of float; two rain days plus the membrane-backorder card and it is critical — at which point the glazing card that arrives later matters twice as much. The debrief question is not whether they saw the announcement, it is whether anyone changed their allocation the week before.
(d) “Weather delays everything equally.” — Stage 3 Rain days stop only weather-flagged work: earthwork, concrete placement, roofing and envelope. The same “ground is soup” card costs three critical days in week 3 and exactly nothing in week 18 with the building dried in. A cure keeps curing through a washout. The forecast is not the decision — the float column is.
(e) “A good outcome means it was a good call.” — Stage 4 The gambler's fallacy in a hard hat, and the most expensive habit on this list. A team pours to plan at column line 4, the roll comes in at 0.61, the inspector takes the pads, and they leave convinced they read the geotech. They took a 45% chance of a three-day hold and $8,500 — and the roll every other team was handed landed above the line.
