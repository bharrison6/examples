Hotter particles hit harder and more often

Double the temperature with the same particles in the same box. Does the pressure double, quadruple, or rise by about 1.4×?

Before you start. In this model temperature is nothing more than the particles' average kinetic energy: hotter means faster. Kinetic energy goes as speed squared, so doubling the temperature raises speeds by only √2 ≈ 1.41. Stage 1 found that pressure counts wall hits; now ask what a faster particle does to each hit, and to how often it hits.

Predict

Temperature 300 to 600, same N, same box. Does pressure go 2×, 4×, or 1.4×?

Try

Commit, then press Run: double the temperature. Watch the speed histogram widen and the wall-hits readout climb.

Takeaway

Temperature is the particles' average kinetic energy, and pressure rises in step with it: hotter particles hit harder and more often.

Check yourself

Doubling the temperature doubled the pressure. Faster particles hit harder — so why not quadruple?

Half the room, twice the hits

Slide the piston in until the box is half as wide, with the thermostat holding the temperature. Does the pressure double?

Before you start. The right wall of the box is a piston that slides in and out. The thermostat is switched on, so the particles keep the same average speed no matter what the piston does. Stages 1 and 2 changed how many particles there are and how fast they go; this stage changes only how far they travel between one wall and the next.

Predict

Box width 100 to 50, same N, thermostat on. Does pressure double, stay the same, or rise by 1.4×?

Try

Commit, then press Run: halve the box. Afterwards, switch the Thermostat off, drag the Piston in yourself, and watch T.

Takeaway

At fixed temperature, pressure × area stays constant: half the room means every wall is hit twice as often.

Check yourself

The box is half as wide, the thermostat held T at 300, and the pressure doubled. Why?

Demo 17 of 17 · Part four · Physics · Stage 4

A law is a prediction, so test it

P·A = N·T predicts every pressure this box should show. Does the measurement agree — and what happens when the particles take up real room?

Before you start. The three stages so far each doubled one thing. The ideal gas law puts them together: pressure × area equals particle count × temperature, P·A = N·T in this model's units. The gauges have shown a ratio all along — measured pressure divided by that prediction. Near 1, the law holds. The law's quiet assumption is that particles take up no room at all; this stage grows them until that stops being true.

Predict

Grow the particles to about three times their radius. Will the measured pressure sit above the law's prediction, on it, or below?

Try

Commit, then press Run: grow the particles. Then use Sweep the box, Sweep temperature and Sweep particle count below to plot the law against the measurements.

Takeaway

The ideal gas law is what tiny, non-interacting particles do on average. It holds here to within the noise, and drifts as soon as the particles take up real room.

Guidehow to use this demo

One question across four stages: where does gas pressure come from, and how far does P·A = N·T get you?

Where this sits: an AI-built classroom tool from Part four. The gas law is never typed into the simulation — pressure is measured from the particles, and the law is a prediction the gauges test.

  1. Add particles. Commit to a prediction, press Run: double the particles, and read the pressure gauge before and after.
  2. Heat it. Same routine with Run: double the temperature; watch the speed histogram widen.
  3. Squeeze it. Run: halve the box with the thermostat on; then switch the Thermostat off and drag the Piston yourself.
  4. Test the law. Run: grow the particles, then plot the law with Sweep the box, Sweep temperature and Sweep particle count.

What is live

The pressure gauge reads the walls, not a formula

Live particle motion, wall hits, pressure, temperature, the histogram

Every particle's position and velocity is advanced on this device many times per frame. When one reaches a wall it bounces, and the momentum it hands over is added to that wall's tally. The pressure gauge is that tally, per second, per unit of wall length. Temperature is computed from the particles' kinetic energy. Nothing is looked up and nothing about the gas law is built in.

Scripted the box, the starting gas and the guided experiment

A 100 × 60 box, 240 particles at a temperature of 300 and a particle radius of 0.35 are chosen teaching values. The guided experiment is a fixed routine: measure for four seconds of simulated time, double N, wait a moment, measure again.

Reasoned the ± band under the pressure reading

A gauge on a few hundred particles is noisy: the band is the reading divided by the square root of the number of wall hits counted, a rough rule of thumb for a random count, not a confidence interval. More particles, more time, or a smaller box all steady it.

How it works

P = (momentum handed to the walls) ÷ (time × total wall length)

A particle of mass 1 moving at speed v⊥ toward a wall leaves with the same speed the other way, so the wall receives momentum 2v⊥. Add every such hit over a stretch of time, divide by that time and by the length of wall, and you have a pressure. Doubling the number of particles doubles the number of hits per second while leaving each hit the same, so the pressure doubles.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Two dimensions. The box is flat, so "pressure" is force per unit length of wall and "volume" is area. Every relationship the stages find holds in 3D too; only the constants change.
  • Reduced units. Every particle has mass 1 and Boltzmann's constant is 1, so temperature is half the mean squared speed and the gas law reads P·A = N·T with no constant in it. The default temperature of 300 is chosen to look like room temperature in kelvin; it is not kelvin.
  • Perfectly elastic walls at rest (the piston aside), which hand back exactly the speed they receive.
  • A finite, noisy gauge. Real gases have ~1023 particles and a gauge that averages over all of them; this box has a few hundred, so the reading wanders. The running average smooths about one second of simulated time.
  • The animation runs at 2× simulated time by default, adjustable from the Speed switch, so that an experiment finishes in a few seconds.

Sources

  • OpenStax Chemistry 2e, §9.5 The Kinetic-Molecular Theory — the postulates this box implements: particles in constant random motion, elastic collisions, pressure arising from collisions with the container walls, and kinetic energy proportional to temperature.
  • OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, §2.2 Pressure, Temperature, and RMS Speed — the derivation of pressure from wall collisions that the gauge here performs numerically.
Boundary: this is an idealized two-dimensional teaching model built to make one mechanism visible. It does not reproduce any real gas's numbers, and its temperature and pressure scales are the model's own.
What is live

The same gauge, with temperature as the variable

Live the temperature readout, the histogram and the pressure

The temperature shown is computed every frame from the particles' actual kinetic energy. The guided experiment rescales every velocity by √2 in one step so the gas is at 600 at once, then measures the walls. The histogram's bars are the particles now; its curve is the 2D Maxwell distribution for the measured temperature, drawn for comparison.

Scripted the thermostat

The thermostat nudges every speed a little toward the target each step, like a water bath the box is sitting in. It changes only the width of the speed distribution. It is a modelling device, not a claim about any particular apparatus.

How it works

T = ½⟨v²⟩   so   vrms = √(2T)

Doubling T multiplies typical speeds by √2. A particle moving √2 times faster hands the wall √2 times the momentum per hit, and comes back to hit it √2 times as often. The product is 2: pressure is proportional to temperature at fixed N and A.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Temperature is defined by kinetic energy alone. These particles have no rotation, no vibration, no internal structure — two degrees of freedom each and nothing else.
  • The velocity distribution is Maxwellian at the start and stays so, because the thermostat rescales uniformly and disc collisions keep it thermalized. Switch collisions off and start all particles at one speed to see a distribution that is not Maxwellian.
  • Every simplification from stage 1 still applies — two dimensions, reduced units, elastic walls, a noisy finite gauge.

Sources

  • OpenStax Chemistry 2e, §9.5 — average kinetic energy proportional to temperature; the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution and how it widens with temperature.
  • The 2D speed density drawn on the histogram, f(v) = (v/T)·e−v²/2T, is the two-dimensional case of that distribution with m = kB = 1; it is computed in this page's model and checked there to integrate to 1, not quoted from a table.
Boundary: the claim is that pressure tracks temperature for a gas of structureless particles. Real molecules store energy in rotation and vibration too, which changes heat capacities but not this proportionality.
What is live

A moving wall, and what it does with the thermostat off

Live the piston, the pressure and the temperature

The piston slides at a fixed 12 units per second toward wherever the slider is set. A particle that bounces off a wall moving at speed u leaves with its normal speed changed by 2u: a wall moving inward speeds it up. That is the entire mechanism by which compressing a gas heats it, and this page computes it for every bounce rather than assuming it.

Scripted the guided experiment holds the thermostat on

The routine measures, slides the piston from width 100 to 50, waits for it to arrive and for the gas to settle, and measures again — with the thermostat holding T at 300 throughout, so that the only thing that changed is the room. Switching the thermostat off is left to you, on purpose.

How it works

P·A = constant  (fixed N and T)

With the same speeds and half the width, a particle crossing the box reaches the piston and the left wall twice as often. The top and bottom walls are shorter, so they are hit as often per unit length as before but there is less of them. Add it up and the total momentum per second per unit of wall doubles. This is Boyle's law, arrived at by counting.

With the thermostat off, the inward-moving piston hands energy to every particle it meets, so T rises and the pressure rises by more than the room alone would give. That is adiabatic compression — the reason a bicycle pump gets warm.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The piston moves at one fixed speed, which is a sizeable fraction of the particles' own speeds. The compression is therefore not slow and gentle, and with the thermostat off it heats the gas more than a slow one would. The direction of the effect is right; its size depends on the piston speed.
  • The thermostat is idealized — it removes energy at a fixed rate everywhere at once. A real water bath conducts heat through the walls with a lag.
  • Every simplification from stages 1 and 2 still applies.

Sources

  • OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, §2.2 — pressure from wall collisions, from which Boyle's law follows at fixed temperature.
  • The moving-wall reflection rule, v′ = −v + 2u, is an exact consequence of an elastic bounce seen from the wall's own frame; it is coded in this page's model, not quoted.
Boundary: this stage shows why pressure and area trade off at fixed temperature, and why compression heats a gas that is not held cold. It reports no real gas's compressibility and no real pump's temperature rise.
What is live, and what is estimated

The sweeps are measured; the size correction is a first-order estimate

Live every sweep point and every ratio

Each sweep steps one of N, T or A through five values. At every step the gas settles for a moment and the walls are measured for three seconds of simulated time; the point plotted is that measurement against N·T / A for the same step. The ratio Z = P·A / (N·T) is measured, not fitted.

Reasonedfirst-order estimate, overstates the effect at higher packing the "expected drift" for big particles

Two particles of radius r cannot bring their centres closer than 2r, so each pair excludes a disc of area 4πr²; sharing that between the two gives about 2πr² of lost room per particle. With N·2πr² of the box unavailable, P ≈ N·T / (A − N·2πr²), so Z ≈ 1 / (1 − 2η) where η is the fraction of the box the particles cover. That is the authors' leading-order estimate from the geometry, offered for direction and rough size. It is not a fitted equation of state.

How it works

Z = P·A ÷ (N·T)   —   1 when the law holds

The ideal gas law assumes particles occupy no space and never interact except at the walls. Small particles with collisions on come close to that: Z sits within a few percent of 1. Grow them and the room they leave each other shrinks, so the walls are hit more often than the law expects and Z climbs. This is the correction a real-gas equation such as van der Waals makes with its b term. There is no attraction between these particles, so the other van der Waals correction, which lowers pressure, does not appear here.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Hard discs, no attraction. Real molecules attract each other weakly at a distance, which pulls Z below 1 at low temperature. This model has only the repulsive core, so it shows one of the two real-gas corrections.
  • Even the "small" default is not zero. At radius 0.35 the particles cover about 1.5% of the box, and Z runs a few percent above 1 for that reason. The demo's "the law holds" is "to within a few percent plus noise", and the gauge shows the number.
  • Sweeps are short. Three simulated seconds per point keeps a sweep to about fifteen real seconds, at the cost of a few percent of scatter on each point.
  • Every simplification from stages 1 to 3 still applies.

Sources

  • OpenStax Chemistry 2e, §9.6 Non-Ideal Gas Behavior — the compressibility factor Z, why molecular volume raises it above 1, and the van der Waals b term this stage's drift corresponds to.
  • The excluded-area estimate above is the authors' own derivation from the disc geometry, as labelled; it is not taken from a source.
Boundary: the claim is that the ideal gas law holds for tiny non-interacting particles and drifts, in a predictable direction, when they take up room. This page does not measure any real gas's compressibility, and its size correction is an estimate of direction and magnitude, not a fitted law.

Learning goals

One per stage, in the learner's own words — these are the four Takeaway cards on screen, verbatim. If a student can say these four things and point at the gauge reading that shows each, the demo has done its job.

  1. Pressure is the running tally of wall hits: twice the particles means twice the hits per second, and twice the pressure.
  2. Temperature is the particles' average kinetic energy, and pressure rises in step with it: hotter particles hit harder and more often.
  3. At fixed temperature, pressure × area stays constant: half the room means every wall is hit twice as often.
  4. The ideal gas law is what tiny, non-interacting particles do on average. It holds here to within the noise, and drifts as soon as the particles take up real room.

Run of show

Every stage follows the same routine: read the question, commit to a prediction (the Run button stays locked until one is chosen), press Run, read the finding. The guided experiment measures for four seconds of simulated time, makes one change, settles, and measures again. At the default 2× speed that is about six real seconds. The numbers below are what the model produces at the defaults; a gauge on a few hundred particles wanders by a few percent run to run, and that is worth saying out loud.

Misconceptions to name

"Crowded particles push harder." Stage 1 and stage 3 both show T unchanged while P doubles. Each hit carries the same momentum; only the count changed. The temperature gauge is the evidence.
"Double the temperature, four times the pressure." Temperature is speed squared, so speeds rise by √2. The hits/s readout rising by about 1.4× while P rises by 2× separates the two factors on screen.
"Collisions between particles make pressure." Switch Collisions off in stage 1: the reading does not change. Particle–particle collisions swap momentum between particles and never touch a wall. (In stage 4 they matter for a different reason: they are what keeps big particles out of each other's space.)
"The law fails, so the simulation is wrong." Stage 4's drift is the point. The ideal gas law is an approximation with a stated assumption, and the gauge shows where the assumption stops holding.

What this model is, and is not

  • Two-dimensional, reduced units. Mass 1, kB = 1, temperature = half the mean squared speed. Every relationship the stages find holds in three dimensions too; only the constants change. The temperature and pressure scales are the model's own.
  • Hard discs with no attraction. The model shows one of the two real-gas corrections (excluded volume, the van der Waals b). The other, attraction pulling Z below 1 at low temperature, is not in it.
  • An idealized thermostat that rescales every speed toward the target, like a water bath with no lag.
  • A piston at one fixed speed, a sizeable fraction of the particles' speeds, so compression with the thermostat off heats more than a slow one would. The direction is right; the size depends on the piston speed.
  • The ± band under the pressure reading is a rule of thumb (reading ÷ √hits), not a confidence interval.
  • The excluded-area estimate in stage 4's Details is the authors' first-order derivation from the disc geometry, labelled as such. It gives direction and rough size, not a fitted equation of state.
