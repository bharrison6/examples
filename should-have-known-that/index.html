You never type an answer. You drag — one slider, one number, one commitment. Forty numbers every engineer is supposed to carry around: yield strengths, ampacities, psi per foot of water. Trust your gut, then find out what it cost you.

Two ways to be wrong

Most sliders are linear and scored on percent error — how close your number is to the real one. A few are marked LOG SCALE (resistivity, the speed of light, the current that stops a heart). Those span orders of magnitude, so they are scored on how many times off you are, not by how many units. On a log question, the right power of ten is most of the battle.

BULLSEYE 100 pts · within 5%  /  1.26× on a log slider
CLOSE 75 pts · within 15%  /  2.1×
WARM 40 pts · within 35%  /  5×
COLD 10 pts · within 60%  /  16×
WAY OFF 0 pts · past that. Gold means you had it, blue means you didn't, red means you weren't in the neighborhood.

The reveal

Lock it in and the truth marker slides out from under your guess to where the number actually lives — so you see the size of the miss, not just the score. Then comes the line that gives the game its name: "you should have known that because…" — the hook that makes the number stick.

Teams

Solo, or 2–4 teams pass-and-play on one phone. Name the teams, and the game hands the device around: a turn card names who's up, the strip across the top keeps the running score, and every team answers the same number of questions. The end screen calls out the sharpest guess of the game and the biggest should-have-known miss — that second one is the debrief.

🎯 Engineering Trivia — Teacher Guide

Should Have Known That · number sense, taught by a slider

What this teaches

Forty verified numbers an engineer is expected to carry without looking up — 8 each across Statics & Mechanics, Materials, Electrical, Fluids & Thermo, and Everyday Engineering. Students never type an answer; they drag a slider and commit. That single design choice is the pedagogy: a slider forces a magnitude out of someone who would otherwise say "I don't know," and a committed wrong answer is what makes the correction stick. Every reveal ends with a one-line "you should have known that because…" anchor, so the number leaves with a reason attached rather than as trivia. Runs offline in any browser — open index.html, phone or projector, nothing to install.

How it is scored

Thirty-five questions use a linear slider scored on percent error. Five use a logarithmic slider, marked LOG SCALE on screen, scored on how many times off you are: copper resistivity, the speed of light, the current that stops a heart, the voltage you can feel as a static shock, and the energy in an AA battery. Those five span orders of magnitude, so percent error would be meaningless — getting the right power of ten is the skill being tested.

20-minute session plan

  • 0–2 minProject the game (⚙ → Presentation mode for projector-sized type). Pick one category and 5 questions. Play the first one yourself, out loud, and be wrong on purpose — it sets the tone that guessing is the assignment.
  • 2–5 minPoll before every lock-in. Hands up for higher, hands up for lower, then press LOCK IT IN. Public commitment before the reveal is the whole mechanism; a guess nobody said out loud costs nothing to be wrong about.
  • 5–14 minSwitch to teams (2–4, pass-and-play on one device, or one device per team) and run 10 questions each. Read the "because…" line aloud after every reveal — do not narrate over the animation, it only takes about two seconds.
  • 14–18 minDebrief from the end screen's "Should have known that" card, which names the game's biggest miss. Ask that team to say what number they thought it was and why. The wrong mental model is more useful to the room than the right answer.
  • 18–20 minReplay one category — PLAY AGAIN from the end screen, or ⚙ → Reset for a clean start screen. Scores jump, and that jump is the point: one exposure with a reason attached is most of what it takes to keep a number.

Solo vs team mode

Solo is the homework and self-quiz mode: one player, a final score out of 100 per question, and the same two end cards. Teams (2–4) is the classroom mode. Name the teams on the start screen — it costs twenty seconds and buys you the hour. The game then hands the device around: a turn card names who is up, a strip across the top carries the running score, and every team answers the same number of questions.

The pool is split evenly, so the question count per team is capped at pool ÷ teams. With all five categories on and four teams, that is 40 ÷ 4 = 10 questions each. The note under the start button tells you the real number before you commit — if you switch categories off, watch it shrink.

One device or many? Pass-and-play on a single phone keeps the room together and is the intended mode. If each team has its own device, run the same category and length on all of them and compare end screens — but the question order is shuffled per device, so the scores are comparable only in aggregate, not question by question.

Discussion prompts

  1. 62.4 lb/ft³ and 0.433 psi/ft are the same fact. Both are in the bank. Ask the room to derive one from the other (62.4 ÷ 144 in²/ft²). One number they already had was two numbers they thought they didn't.
  2. Which number is the dangerous one — 100 mA across the chest, or the 3,000 V it takes to feel a static shock? A doorknob zap is 5–25 kV and harmless. It is the current, not the voltage, that kills.
  3. Why not build everything out of aluminum? Young's modulus 200 vs 70 GPa; density 7,850 vs 2,700 kg/m³. Both ratios are about 2.9, so stiffness-to-weight is nearly identical — the real choice is cost, buckling and joining.
  4. What if steel and concrete expanded at different rates? They both run near 12 µm/m·°C. Every summer would crack the building. Reinforced concrete works on a coincidence.
  5. Did the steel melt? Carbon steel melts near 1,500 °C, but it softens enough to fail far below that. Good for separating "melted" from "failed" in students' vocabulary.
  6. A36 is named after its own answer — the 36 is the yield strength in ksi (≈250 MPa). How many other "memorized" numbers are printed on the spec sheet name?
  7. One gallon of gasoline is 33.7 kWh — roughly a full day of an average US home's electricity. Best single number in the bank for an energy conversation.
  8. Why did the log questions go so badly? Because they are not recall questions. Tell the class this in advance; it lowers the cost of guessing and makes the failure informative instead of embarrassing.
Reading a Way Off: a Way Off on a linear question is a fact the student does not have. A Way Off on a log question is a bigger problem — they do not know what size the answer is. Say the difference out loud; it is the single most transferable idea in the game.

Editing the question bank

Every question is one object in a commented array at the top of index.html — search for QUESTION BANK. The fields are id, category, prompt, units, value (the true answer), min/max (slider endpoints), scale ("linear" or "log") and factoid (the "because…" line). Edit in place with any text editor; the game itself has no build step.

  • New categories appear on the start screen and get a color automatically — just type a new category string.
  • Keep the true value off-center in [min, max], and keep the range wide enough that the endpoints don't give the answer away.
  • Use "log" whenever plausible student guesses span orders of magnitude. Both min and max must be greater than zero on a log slider.
  • Always write the factoid. A number without a reason is trivia, and trivia does not survive the week.

One region of index.html is generated: the presenter notes, between the __GUIDE__ markers. They are this guide, injected by tools/guide-sync.js, so the on-screen notes and the printed page cannot disagree. Edit teacher-guide.html, then run node tools/guide-sync.js and node tools/pdf.mjs.

Classroom logistics

  • Nothing to install and nothing saved. One file, no network, no accounts, no stored state — and ⚙ → Reset puts the game back to a fresh load between class periods without reloading the page.
  • ⚙ Settings holds Open Presenter Notes (this guide, on screen), Presentation mode and Reset. ? is the Guide button: the same overlay opens on every load, so students who arrive late can read the rules themselves, and ? reopens it any time.
  • Keys: Enter or Space locks in a guess and advances the reveal. Esc closes any popup. Everything else is touch.
  • The reveal runs about two seconds: the truth marker slides out from under the guess, the band lands, then the "because…" line fades in and NEXT appears. Budget roughly 30–45 seconds per question with discussion.
