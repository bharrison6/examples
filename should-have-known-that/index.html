Guidehow to use this demo

Forty numbers an engineer is expected to carry without looking up — can you place them by feel?

Where this sits: Part four's example projects apply an AI-built classroom tool with its goal, engine and assumptions all one tap away — this one is a calibration game for number sense.

  1. Linear estimates. Pick your length, categories and mode, then press Start linear estimates; drag the slider and press Lock it in for each question.
  2. Log estimates. Unlocks once stage 1 is complete — five questions that span orders of magnitude, scored differently on purpose.
  3. Debrief. Unlocks once stage 2 is complete — final scores and the linear-vs-log comparison.

Getting around

The stage tabs switch stages, but stages 2 and 3 stay locked until you have played the one before — the tabs stay visible so you can see the whole route. Details opens the evidence and sources for the current stage. Guide reopens this card; Settings has Open Presenter Notes, Presentation mode and Reset.

Time

A 10-question linear round plus the 5-question log round runs about 15–20 minutes with discussion; solo or 2–4 team pass-and-play on one device.

Sourced all 35 linear values below

Every number in this stage is a published reference value, a defined constant, a code requirement, or an exact unit conversion — not a measurement this page performs. Fourteen of them ship with a qualifier because a single number would otherwise stand in for a real range, a specified minimum, or a design convention.

How it works

error = |guess − true| / true  ·  bands at 5% / 15% / 35% / 60%

Percent error is the fraction your guess misses the true value by. Below 5% is Bullseye (100 pts); past 60% is Way Off (0 pts). The full table is in the printable guide.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Every value is a typical or reference figure for its category, not a measurement of one specific real object — a real beam, a real cord, a real gallon will differ, sometimes by a lot, depending on grade, brand, or conditions.
  • Several values are design conventions fixed by a code rather than physical averages (steel's E = 200 GPa, concrete's 150 pcf design weight, A36's 36 ksi minimum yield) — using the code number is correct engineering practice, but it is a different kind of "true" than a measured physical constant, which is exactly what each item's qualifier says.
  • Slider endpoints are chosen to be wide enough not to give the answer away; they are not themselves a claim about the plausible range of the true value.

Sources

      Engineering Trivia — Should Have Known That

      You need: index.html open in any modern browser, phone or projector. No network, no install, no accounts. Nothing is fetched at runtime, so it works with the wi-fi off.

      Learning goals

      One per stage, in the learner's own words — these are the three Takeaway cards on screen, verbatim.

      1. A confident guess you commit to is what makes a correction stick — a shrug does not.
      2. On a log scale, the order of magnitude is most of the answer — and item 23's rule-of-thumb number is a single point standing in for a whole risk curve.
      3. A ranged or code-defined number is still worth a confident guess — precision and truth are not the same thing.

      Where this sits on the path

      An AI-built classroom tool from Part four: its goal is number-sense calibration, its engine is a live-scored slider running entirely on the device, and its assumptions — which of its 40 values are single facts versus ranges, minimums, or code conventions — are one tap away in each stage's Details drawer.

      One question for the session

      "If you had to bet, not guess — how close is your gut, really, to the numbers you already use?"

      Stage 1 · Linear estimates

      Question. Which of the numbers an engineer is supposed to carry around can you actually place by feel?

      Predict. Drag the slider to where you think each of 35 numbers sits, before the truth is revealed.

      Activity (timed beats). 0–2 minPick a length (5/10/15), categories, and solo or teams; play the first one yourself, wrong on purpose, to set the tone. 2–10 minPoll before every lock-in if presenting to a room: hands up for higher, hands up for lower, then press LOCK IT IN.       Expected observation. The band badge (Bullseye/Close/Warm/Cold/Way Off) and the "you vs. truth" markers on the track; the observation cue names the band and the miss size.

      Teaching limit. Percent-error scoring only makes sense while the true value and the guess are on the same, roughly-linear scale — which is exactly what breaks in stage 2.

      Stage 2 · Log estimates

      Question. When a guess can be off by a factor of ten, does the exact digit even matter?

      Predict. Commit to a power of ten first, on all five log-scale items (resistivity, the speed of light, the cardiac-fibrillation current, the static-shock voltage, and AA battery energy).

      Activity. 10–14 minPlay all five; ask the room to say a power of ten out loud before each lock-in rather than a specific number.

      Expected observation. A big absolute miss can still land Bullseye if the order of magnitude is right — the opposite of stage 1's intuition.

      Check-yourself answer key. "No — 100 mA is one point on a risk curve, not a safety line" is correct; treating 40 mA as automatically safe because it is under 100 mA is the refutation item's flawed model.

      Teaching limit. The game cannot show WHY the curve looks the way it does (current × duration × path) — only that a single number understates it. The Details drawer names the shape; it does not re-derive IEC 60479-1's curve.

      Misconceptions this bank can leave, refuted

      Misconception: "100 mA is the safe/unsafe line for shock." It is a rule-of-thumb anchor on a current×duration×path curve (IEC 60479-1); sustained exposure carries meaningful fibrillation risk from well below 100 mA. Item 23's qualifier and stage 2's refutation check both name this directly — it is the single highest-stakes item in the bank.
      Misconception: "A36's 250 MPa is what the steel actually measures." It is ASTM A36/A36M's specified MINIMUM; real mill-certified steel commonly tests higher. Stage 1's check item exists to break exactly this substitution of a floor for a fact.
      Misconception: "7¾ in risers are the code, full stop." That figure is the IRC's residential cap; the IBC's general means-of-egress cap is 7 in, and only Exception 3 for dwelling units raises it to match. Carrying the residential number into a commercial context applies the wrong code to the wrong building type.
      Misconception: "13 A is safe for any 16-gauge extension cord." NEC Table 400.5(A)(1) sets 13 A by conductor count and ambient temperature, not length; a widely used practical derating guide drops well below 13 A for a long cord run. The bank's own factoid already warns a 1,500 W heater is close to the derated limit — a long cord makes it worse, not the same.

      Discussion prompts

      1. 62.4 lb/ft³ and 0.433 psi/ft are the same fact. Ask the room to derive one from the other (62.4 ÷ 144 in²/ft²).
      2. Which number is the dangerous one — 100 mA across the chest, or the 3,000 V it takes to feel a static shock? It is the current, not the voltage, that kills; say plainly that 100 mA is a rule of thumb, not a safe/unsafe line.
      3. Why not build everything out of aluminum? Young's modulus 200 vs. 70 GPa; density 7,850 vs. 2,700 kg/m³. Both ratios are about 2.9, so stiffness-to-weight is nearly identical — the real choice is cost, buckling, and joining.
      4. What if steel and concrete expanded at different rates? Every summer would crack the building — reinforced concrete works on an approximate coincidence, not an exact one.
      5. A36 is named after its own answer — the 36 is the specified minimum yield strength in ksi. How many other "memorized" numbers are printed on the spec sheet's name, and how many of those are minimums rather than typicals?
      6. A single number can be right AND still teach the wrong lesson. Every one of the 40 values in this bank checks out against a real source — but 17 of them ship with a qualifier because a bare number would have implied more precision than the world actually has. Ask the room to name one more everyday "fact" that is really a range, a floor, or a design convention.
      7. One gallon of gasoline is 33.7 kWh — roughly a full day of an average US home's electricity, and (per this build's re-sourcing) a genuinely accurate figure for real gasoline, not a lowball regulatory number.
      8. Why did the log questions go so badly (or so well)? Because they are not recall questions — getting the size of the number right is the actual skill.
      Reading a Way Off: a Way Off on a linear question is a fact the student does not have. A Way Off on a log question means they do not know what size the answer is — say the difference out loud; it is the most transferable idea in the game.

      Thirty-five linear questions score on percent error; five log questions (stage 2) score on decades off — |log10(guess/true)|.

      Editing the question bank

      The bank now lives in src/data.js (moved out of index.html for this retrofit) — each entry carries id, category, prompt, units, value, min/max, scale ("linear"/"log"), factoid, and now an optional qualifier plus a source object (name, url, fetched). Edit src/data.js, then run node build.js. A value with no qualifier is presented as a single, unhedged fact — add one whenever a new question is really a range, a specified minimum, or a code/edition-dependent figure, per the standing "research, not memory" rule: back it with an actual source before shipping it, or mark it unverified.
