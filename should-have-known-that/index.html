Should Have Known ThatHow to play

You never type an answer. You drag — one slider, one number, one commitment. Forty numbers every engineer is supposed to carry around: yield strengths, ampacities, psi per foot of water. Trust your gut, then find out what it cost you.

Two ways to be wrong

Most sliders are linear and scored on percent error — how close your number is to the real one. A few are marked LOG SCALE (resistivity, the speed of light, the current that stops a heart). Those span orders of magnitude, so they are scored on how many times off you are, not by how many units. On a log question, the right power of ten is most of the battle.

BULLSEYE 100 pts · within 5%  /  1.26× on a log slider
CLOSE 75 pts · within 15%  /  2.1×
WARM 40 pts · within 35%  /  5×
COLD 10 pts · within 60%  /  16×
WAY OFF 0 pts · past that. Gold means you had it, blue means you didn't, red means you weren't in the neighborhood.

The reveal

Lock it in and the truth marker slides out from under your guess to where the number actually lives — so you see the size of the miss, not just the score. Then comes the line that gives the game its name: "you should have known that because…" — the hook that makes the number stick.

Teams

Solo, or 2–4 teams pass-and-play on one phone. Name the teams, and the game hands the device around: a turn card names who's up, the strip across the top keeps the running score, and every team answers the same number of questions. The end screen calls out the sharpest guess of the game and the biggest should-have-known miss — that second one is the debrief.

Stage notes · 10–20 minutesPresenter's notes

Before you start

Turn on Presentation mode if you're projecting. Pick the length on the start screen: 5 questions is a warm-up, 10 is a class block, 15 is the full set. Categories are toggles — switch off the ones you haven't taught and the pool shrinks accordingly (the note under the buttons tells you how many questions are actually in play, and caps the count if you've asked for more than the pool holds).

For teams, the pool is split evenly: with 4 teams and every category on, that's 40 ÷ 4 = 10 questions each. Ask the teams to name themselves — it costs 20 seconds and buys you the whole hour.

Running it

0:00
Project question one and say nothing. Let someone drag the slider while the room watches the number move. The dragging is the lesson — they are being asked to commit to a magnitude, out loud, in front of people.
"Nobody is asking you to be right. You're being asked to be in the right neighborhood."
before
each lock
Freeze it. Before anyone presses LOCK IT IN, poll the room: hands up for higher, hands up for lower. Commitment before the reveal is what makes the miss memorable — a guess nobody said out loud costs nothing to be wrong about.
reveal
The truth marker slides for about a second, the band lands, then the "because…" line fades in and NEXT appears — roughly two seconds end to end. Do not narrate over it. Read the anchor line aloud after it appears, then move.
after
a WAY OFF
Distinguish the two failures out loud. Way off on a linear question is a fact you don't have. Way off on a log question is a much bigger problem: you don't know what size the answer is. That's the one worth stopping for.
end
The end screen names the sharpest guess and the biggest should-have-known miss. Debrief from the miss card, not the score.

Discussion hooks that are actually in the bank

  • 62.4 lb/ft³ and 0.433 psi/ft are the same fact. Both are in the bank (Statics, Fluids). Run them in the same game and ask the room to derive one from the other — 62.4 ÷ 144 in²/ft² = 0.433. One number they already knew was two numbers they thought they didn't.
  • Voltage doesn't kill; current does. The Electrical set has ~100 mA across the chest causing fibrillation, and ~3,000 V as the floor for feeling a static shock — a doorknob zap is 5–25 kV and harmless. Ask which of the two numbers is the dangerous one, then show both.
  • Steel vs aluminum. Young's modulus 200 vs 70 GPa, density 7,850 vs 2,700 kg/m³. Both ratios are about 2.9. Ask why we don't just build everything out of aluminum — the answer is that stiffness-to-weight is nearly identical, so the choice is made on cost, buckling and joining, not stiffness.
  • Steel and concrete expand at the same rate (~12 µm/m·°C). Ask the room what would happen to reinforced concrete if they didn't. Every summer would crack the building.
  • Steel doesn't melt in a building fire. Carbon steel melts near 1,500 °C — but it softens enough to fail long before that. Good for separating "melted" from "failed" in students' vocabulary.
  • The log questions are the real diagnostic. Copper resistivity 1.7×10⁻⁸ Ω·m and the speed of light are not recall questions — they are "do you know how big this kind of thing is" questions. Expect the class to do badly and say so in advance; it lowers the cost of guessing.
  • A36's name is its answer. The 36 is the yield strength in ksi (≈250 MPa). Worth pointing out that half the materials numbers engineers "memorize" are printed on the spec sheet name.
  • One gallon of gasoline is 33.7 kWh — about a full day of an average US home's electricity. The best single number in the bank for an energy conversation.

If you have five more minutes

Replay the same category in teams. The second pass scores far better, and that jump is the point: these are not facts anyone is born with, and one exposure with a reason attached is most of what it takes to keep one.
