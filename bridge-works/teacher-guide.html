Bridge Works — Teacher Guide

Murray State University · Statics you can watch fail

What students do

Drag members between grid joints and drive a vehicle across. Costing follows a fabricator's bill: steel by weight (9.4 kg/m at $4.80/kg ≈ $45/m) plus $180 per joint for the gusset, bolts and labour. So the cheapest bridge that survives wins, golf-style against par. Members are coloured live — red = tension, blue = compression, brighter = nearer breaking — so the load path is visible before anything fails. Offline in any browser: open index.html (Chromebook-friendly, mouse or touch).

Underneath is a real direct stiffness solver: element stiffnesses assembled into a global K, supports as boundary conditions, K·u = F solved for joint displacements, member forces from the strains. Overloaded members are deleted and the structure re-solved, so failures cascade like real ones.

30-minute session plan

  • 0–4Project it (⚙ → Projector mode). On Level 1 build only the flat roadway and press Load test. It folds. Name what they just saw: joint, member, tension, compression, support. Add a post and two struts; run it again.
  • 4–10Students play Level 1 in pairs with X-ray on. Have them use the Joint tool: tap a joint, see every force meeting there and their sum. It is always zero. That panel is the method of joints.
  • 10–18Level 2 — the lesson. It opens with a rectangular frame already built. Test it first: it folds into a parallelogram and the debrief reports a mechanism, naming the joints that ran away. Take predictions, then let them add diagonals. Par forces a second pass: which members do nothing?
  • 18–25Level 3 — Heavy Haul. Four times the load. Point them at the debrief's “buckles at” column to discover that compression rating depends on length and tension rating does not.
  • 25–30Project a student's debrief. Load Pratt, Howe and Warren from the Gallery at equal depth and compare. Post the leaderboard. Levels 4–6 make good homework.

Why connections are priced

A flat price per metre makes extra panels free, which is false and hides a real design driver. Here connections are roughly 45% of a typical bill, so panel count is a genuine trade-off: bracing a panel you already have costs steel only, while adding a panel costs a joint as well. That is exactly why real trusses use as few panels as they can. The debrief splits the bill into steel and connections.

Axial members

Every joint is a pin, so it carries no bending moment. A straight member pinned at both ends is therefore a two-force member: its only possible force is along its own axis — a pull (tension) or a push (compression). One number describes it completely. Each joint satisfies ΣFx = ΣFy = 0; the Joint tool draws exactly that and prints the residual.

Tension vs. compression

Same steel section throughout (E = 200 GPa, A = 12 cm²). Pulled, it yields at Tmax = σyA = 300 kN at any length. Pushed, it does not crush — it buckles sideways, at

Pcr = π²EI / L² ≈ 720 kN·m² / L²

capped at 180 kN when stubby. Length is squared: double a strut, quarter its capacity.

Students can find the consequence themselves: flip a working design below the deck so the long members hang instead of prop. Same geometry, same cost, far lower stress.

Why triangles

Three side lengths fix a triangle completely — its corners cannot move without stretching a side. Four do not fix a quadrilateral: it shears into a parallelogram with every side the same length as before. A member only resists a change in its own length, so a rectangular panel meets that shearing with zero resistance.

In the solver this is arithmetic, not a special case: the shearing motion strains nothing, so K maps it to zero force and is singular. The game finds the rank deficiency, reads the null-space direction out of the elimination, and folds the bridge along that exact direction — the collapse students watch is the real mathematical failure mode. During the build phase it reports how many independent folds exist (the dimension of that null space) and animates each in turn. A null vector has zero strain only to first order, so the drawn shape is projected back onto exact member lengths before display — what the class watches really does keep every member at its built length. Adding one well-placed member removes exactly one fold: count down to zero.

Static determinacy, plainly

Count members m, reactions r, joints j; each joint gives 2 equations.

  • m + r < 2j — mechanism. Not enough members. It folds; strength cannot help.
  • m + r = 2j — determinate. Equilibrium alone gives every force, and every member is critical: lose one and the rest is a mechanism.
  • m + r > 2j — indeterminate. Spare members; load now splits by stiffness, not equilibrium alone. This is why the game solves K·u = F instead of walking joint to joint.

The count is necessary but not sufficient — members in the wrong places leave a local mechanism. The solver checks the matrix, not the count.

Misconception 1 — “more members always makes it stronger.” Extra members change how load shares, and a stiffer path attracts more force, so an added member can overload its neighbour. And every member costs money: the debrief flags those that never passed 5% of their limit, and first attempts usually have several. Try it: add a member to a design that just made par and watch the peak move.
Misconception 2 — “the verticals do nothing, they're spacers.” In a Pratt the verticals are in compression carrying each panel's shear to the top chord; in a Howe they are in tension. Try it: load a Pratt from the Gallery, turn on X-ray, erase one vertical — its panel becomes a rectangle and the truss becomes a mechanism. A member genuinely carrying nothing is drawn grey, and that is a different thing.
Misconception 3 — “tension and compression are just plus and minus.” The same 4 m member holds 300 kN pulled and 45 kN pushed. The sign is not bookkeeping; it selects a completely different failure mode. Try it: note a design's peak use, then rebuild it upside down under the deck.

Discussion questions

  1. The bare rectangle folded without any member changing length. Where did the load go, and why could no member resist it?
  2. Two designs, same cost and same joints, but one props the deck from above and one hangs it from below. Predict which is safer, then check. What in Pcr = π²EI/L² explains it?
  3. A Warren's diagonals alternate tension–compression all the way along. Take one bottom joint and use ΣFy = 0 to show why its two diagonals must have opposite signs.
  4. Why is the bottom chord's force greatest at mid-span and near zero at the supports?
  5. One member snapped and the whole bridge came down. What does that say about its spare load paths? What would you add, and what would it cost?
  6. Level 5's pier drops par sharply. Halving a span does much more than halve the forces — why? Where do real bridges use this, and when can't they?
  7. Turn on Self-weight and retest a design that just made par. What happens, and why does it matter more as spans grow?
