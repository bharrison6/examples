A shape that cannot hold still

Why does a flat roadway fold under a car before any member is overloaded?

Before you start. A truss is a frame of straight members meeting at joints. Every joint here is a pin — it carries no bending — so a member can only be pulled (tension) or pushed (compression) along its own length. A mechanism is a frame that can change shape without any member changing length. Demo 7 handed forward one habit: commit a prediction before you run anything, so the run is a measurement and not a show.

Predict

Level 1 opens with nothing but the gap. Lay the flat roadway alone and call it under the canvas: does it hold, snap, buckle — or fold?

Takeaway

A frame with a four-sided panel folds without straining any member; a triangle cannot change shape without changing a member's length — and the solver finds the fold as a rank deficiency, not as a rule someone typed in.

Length decides the push

The same 4 m member holds 300 kN pulled. Why does it fail at 45 kN pushed?

Before you start. Yield is the stress at which steel stops springing back; pulled, every member here yields at σy·A = 300 kN whatever its length. Buckling is a pushed member bowing sideways long before it could crush, and Euler's formula Pcr = π²EI/L² says the load that does it falls with the square of length. Stage 1 gave you shapes that hold still; this stage asks which of their members are doing the pushing.

Predict

Level 3 sends a 125 kN truck over a 10 m gap. Before you test your first design there, call the first failure: fold, buckle, tear, or hold?

Try

Build, or load a Gallery truss; turn on Numbers; commit your call; press ▶ Load test; then read Weakest link under the canvas and the debrief's buckles at column.

Takeaway

Compression capacity falls as 1/L² — 180 kN at 2 m, 80 kN at 3 m, 45 kN at 4 m — while tension stays at 300 kN at any length, which is why cheap designs shorten their struts or hang the deck so the long members are pulled.

Where the load travels

How does a wheel's weight find its way to the ground when you cannot build below the deck, or when a pier appears mid-span?

Before you start. A load path is the chain of members that carries a wheel's weight to a support — an abutment or a pier. In a through truss the roadway is the bottom chord and the frame stands above it; in a bowstring a curved compression chord is tied by the deck, so the deck itself is in tension. Stage 2 told you which members are dangerous; this stage moves the load onto different ones.

Predict

Level 4 forbids building below the deck. Call the first failure of your first design there — fold, buckle, tear, or hold — before you press the button.

Try

Load a Gallery bowstring or Pratt at 2–3 m depth, use Check a joint on a deck joint to read ΣF = 0, commit your call and press ▶ Load test. Then move to Level 5 and see what the pier does to par.

Takeaway

Load splits by stiffness, not by counting: a stiffer path attracts more force, a mid-span pier turns one 16 m span into two 8 m ones and cuts the chord forces far more than in half, and a member drawn grey is steel you paid for that carries nothing.

What this page actually computes

Live the canvas, the colours, the joint tool, the fold

Every member force on screen comes from assembling this design's stiffness matrix and solving K·u = F in your browser, again on every edit. The dashed fold you watch on a rectangle is not an animation someone drew: it is the null-space direction the solver found when K came out singular, projected back onto exact member lengths so nothing stretches.

Measureddeterministic — no seed; 86 checks in test-physics.js the pars

Each par is a budget a triangulated design can beat, found offline against this same solver: Level 1's best surviving design in the search costs $929 against a par of $1,050, and no un-triangulated ladder survives any level at any depth or panel count (80 variants tried per level). Nothing in the solver is random, so re-running the suite reproduces every figure.

Illustrative the bill

Steel at $4.80/kg on a 9.42 kg/m section (about $45 a metre) and $180 per joint are invented so that panel count is a real trade-off. The shape of the bill — connections roughly 45% of a typical design — is the teaching point; the dollar figures are not a quotation.

How it works

k = (EA/L)·[c² cs; cs s²]  →  K·u = F

The direct stiffness method: each member is a two-force axial element whose stiffness EA/L is rotated into global coordinates by the cosines of its direction, every element is added into a global matrix K, the supports are applied as boundary conditions, and the displacements u are solved for. Member forces follow from the strains. Because it is a stiffness solve and not a joint-by-joint walk, designs with extra members, two pinned ends or a pier — statically indeterminate designs — solve correctly, with load split by stiffness.

The reduced matrix is factored by Gaussian elimination with full pivoting, which reports the rank honestly. Rank deficiency means a motion that strains nothing exists; the solver returns that null-space vector, and the game projects it onto exact member lengths (largest amplitude with under 0.5% length change) before drawing it.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Two-dimensional, pin-jointed. No bending in any member, no out-of-plane behaviour.
  • Linear elastic, small displacement. Deflections are drawn 45× life size (capped at 1.3 m so a near-failure design stays on screen) but solved at true scale.
  • One steel section for everything, so the design problem is about geometry rather than shopping for sections.
  • Ideal supports. The left abutment is a pin (fixed both ways); the others are rollers (fixed vertically, free to slide).
  • A joint dropped mid-member splits it, and two members meeting in a straight line at an unloaded joint are condensed back into one (springs in series, EA/Ltotal, exact) — otherwise a pure pin idealisation would call every such joint a mechanism.
  • Members no longer than 4 m, on a 0.5 m grid.
  • Joints never fail. This is a steel truss with idealised pins: member buckling and yield are modelled, connection failure is not. See the Reasoned note below.
Reasoned why the joints are idealised, and what that leaves out

In real structures — especially glued or nailed ones like a craft-stick bridge — the connection is often what goes first, and this model cannot represent that failure at all. The steel pin-jointed idealisation is chosen precisely because it isolates the two things statics can teach cleanly: a shape that cannot hold still, and a member that buckles or yields. That choice is the honest one for this lesson, and the reason engineers model what they model is a lesson in itself.

What this page actually computes

Live every member's force and its percentage of limit

Utilisation is force divided by capacity, and capacity is chosen per member per sign: σyA when pulled, min(0.6σyA, π²EI/L²) when pushed, with L the whole unbraced straight run. The debrief's buckles at column is that second figure.

Sourcedspecified minimum, not typical; the model rounds 36 ksi to 250 MPa the yield stress

ASTM A36 carbon structural steel is specified at a minimum yield of 36 ksi (≈ 248 MPa). Real A36 typically yields somewhat higher; the game uses 250 MPa as the round figure, so Tmax = 250 MPa × 12 cm² = 300 kN.

Sourcedreference-table value, not a standard E = 200 GPa

The modulus for structural steel in common engineering reference tables. With I = 3.647 × 10−7 m4, EI = 72.9 kN·m² and π²EI ≈ 720 kN·m², which is where the 720/L² on the Takeaway card comes from.

Reasonednot sourced on this page the section and the density

A 12 cm² section, I = 3.647 × 10−7 m4 and 7,850 kg/m³ are the authors' choices: the density is the usual carbon-steel figure, and the section was picked so that a 2 m strut sits right at the 180 kN cap while a 4 m strut carries a quarter of that. Nothing on the Takeaway card depends on the exact values — only on the 1/L² law.

How it works

Pcr = π²EI / L² ≈ 720 kN·m² / L², capped at 180 kN

Euler buckling for a pinned-pinned column. The cap is 0.6 of the squash load: a simplification standing in for the real column curves, which bend the Euler hyperbola over as a strut gets stubby. So 2 m → 180 kN (the cap binds from about 2.0 m), 3 m → 80 kN, 4 m → 45 kN. The buckling length is the whole collinear chain, because an intermediate pin with nothing bracing it braces nothing.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Pinned-pinned effective length (K = 1) for every member; no end fixity credit.
  • A straight, perfect member. Real struts have initial crookedness and residual stress, which lower the buckling load below Euler's.
  • No lateral bracing out of plane. The one length is used for both directions.
  • The 0.6 cap is a stand-in for a design column curve, not a code provision.
  • Tension capacity is yield, not fracture, and no connection or net-section check is made at the joints — a joint here cannot fail, where in a glued or bolted real structure the connection is often the first thing to go.

Sources

  • Purdue ME 323, Mechanics of Materials, Topic 18: Column buckling (PDF) — read 2026-09-16: eq. (7), "Pcr = π²EI/L²" for the pinned-pinned column, and eq. (9) with the effective length Leff for other end conditions.
  • SSAB, ASTM A36 product page — read 2026-09-16: "Yield strength (min ksi) 36"; tensile strength 58–80 ksi. A steelmaker's datasheet, not the ASTM standard itself, which is paywalled.
  • The Engineering ToolBox, Young's Modulus table — read 2026-09-16: "Steel, Structural ASTM-A36: 200 GPa". A reference table; treat as a widely used value rather than a measurement.
Boundary: the 1/L² law is the lesson; the 45 kN is a property of this section. A real design would use column curves, effective-length factors, safety factors and a real section catalogue, and would get different numbers. What transfers is the direction and the shape of the effect, not its size.
What is live

What this page actually computes

Live the crossing, re-solved at every position

The vehicle is stepped across the deck and the structure is solved from scratch at each position with whatever members are still standing. Each wheel load lands on the deck member beneath it and is shared to that member's two joints by lever arm — the standard tributary distribution to panel points. The regression run checks that every newton of wheel load reaches the joints at 48 positions.

Live the progressive collapse

When a member's utilisation passes 100% it is removed and the structure is re-solved with what is left, up to forty times per position. On a minimal truss the first break is fatal — what remains is a mechanism. On a redundant one the run records a genuine cascade (buckle → re-solve → tear → re-solve → mechanism).

Measureddeterministic — no seed Pratt verticals compress, Howe verticals stretch, Warren diagonals alternate

The gallery's one-line descriptions of where each truss puts tension and compression are checked against this solver in the regression suite, not asserted from a textbook: Pratt interior diagonals in tension, Howe in compression, a Warren alternating T/C with one repeat exactly at mid-span and a fully tensile bottom chord.

Sourced the gallery's dates

Howe 1840, Whipple's iron bowstring 1841, Pratt 1844: the US patents, read today. Warren 1848: a British patent, read through a professional-magazine history rather than the patent itself — see Sources.

How it works

at each x: F(x) → solve → break the worst → re-solve → next x

A quasi-static moving load: no inertia, no dynamic amplification, no impact. That is the usual first idealisation for a slow vehicle, and it is what lets every position be a clean equilibrium solve. A member dangling off a half-built structure with nothing pushing on it is pruned as a zero-force member and drawn grey rather than reported as a collapse; with Dead load on, every live member first receives its own weight (900 N/m, half to each end), so a hanger keeps its load and a stub that cannot carry its own weight is reported honestly.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Quasi-static. No dynamics, so no impact factor and no vibration.
  • Deck members carry wheels between joints without bending — the wheel load goes straight to the two joints.
  • One member breaks per re-solve, the most overloaded one; simultaneous failures are sequenced.
  • Removal is instantaneous and the remaining structure is solved statically; the falling-wreckage animation after a mechanism is a display, not a solve.
  • The self-weight figure (900 N/m) stands for the member plus its share of a deck and is illustrative.

It shows that a spare load path is what turns "one member gone" into "still standing", and that the spare path costs money.

One question for the session

Ask it before anyone touches a control; return to it at the debrief.

"When a bridge fails, which piece goes first — and could you have called it before the truck rolled on?"

Most of the room expects the thinnest-looking member to snap. The demo's answer is that the frame usually folds before anything snaps, and when something does go it is a long pushed member, not a pulled one. Both surprises are the statics.

Stage 1 · Mechanisms (Levels 1–2)

Question. Why does a flat roadway fold under a car before any member is overloaded?

Predict prompt. Lay the flat roadway alone and call it: does it hold, snap, buckle, or fold?

Activity. ~2 minDraw the deck; call; test. ~4 minLevel 2's frame: call; test; add diagonals until the cue reads Rigid; test again.

Expected observation. The flat deck and the rectangle both fold with every member at 0% of its limit. The debrief's How it moved table names the joints that ran away. One diagonal per four-sided panel removes exactly one fold; the cue counts them down.

Check-yourself key. The fold changed no member's length, and a member only resists a change in its own length. "Too weak" and "loose joints" are both refuted: strength never entered it, and the pins are ideal.

Teaching limit. Every joint is an ideal pin. Real connections carry some bending and can fail; glued or nailed models usually fail at the joint, which this solver cannot show.

Misconceptions

"More members always makes it stronger." Load shares by stiffness, so an added member can pull force onto a neighbour and overload it, and every member costs steel and a joint. The debrief flags members under 5% of limit. Try it: add one to a design that just made par and watch the peak move.
"The verticals do nothing — they're spacers." In a Pratt they carry each panel's shear in compression; in a Howe, in tension. A member genuinely carrying nothing is drawn grey, and that is a different thing. Try it: erase one vertical with X-ray on.
"Tension and compression are just plus and minus." The same 4 m member holds 300 kN pulled and 45 kN pushed. The sign selects a different failure mode. Try it: rebuild a design upside down under the deck.
"It failed because the steel was too weak." On Levels 1 and 2 the frame folds with every member at 0% of its limit. Strength cannot fix a mechanism; a diagonal can.

Why connections are priced

Costing follows a fabricator's bill: steel by weight (9.4 kg/m at $4.80/kg, about $45 a metre) plus $180 per joint for the gusset, bolts and labour. A flat price per metre makes extra panels free, which hides a real design driver; here connections run roughly 45% of a typical bill, so bracing a panel you already have costs steel only while adding a panel costs a joint too. The figures are illustrative; the shape of the bill is the point.

Static determinacy, plainly

Count members m, reactions r, joints j; each joint gives two equations. m + r < 2j: a mechanism — it folds and strength cannot help. m + r = 2j: determinate — every member is critical. m + r > 2j: indeterminate — load now splits by stiffness, which is why the game solves K·u = F instead of walking joint to joint. The count is necessary, not sufficient: members in the wrong places leave a local mechanism, and the solver checks the matrix, not the count.

Discussion questions

  1. The bare rectangle folded without any member changing length. Where did the load go, and why could no member resist it?
  2. Two designs, same cost and same joints: one props the deck from above, one hangs it from below. Predict which is safer, then check. What in Pcr = π²EI/L² explains it?
  3. A Warren's diagonals alternate tension–compression along its length. Take one bottom joint and use ΣFy = 0 to show why its two diagonals must have opposite signs.
  4. Why is the bottom chord's force greatest at mid-span and near zero at the supports?
  5. One member snapped and the whole bridge came down. What does that say about its spare load paths, and what would a spare path cost?
  6. Turn on Dead load and retest a design that just made par. What happens, and why does it matter more as spans grow?
