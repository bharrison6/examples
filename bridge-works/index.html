Determinacy, if you want it on the board

Members m, reactions r, joints j; each joint gives 2 equations.

  • m + r < 2j — mechanism. Not enough members; strength cannot help.
  • m + r = 2j — determinate. Every member is critical: lose one and the rest is a mechanism.
  • m + r > 2j — indeterminate. Load splits by stiffness, not equilibrium alone — which is exactly why the game solves K·u = F instead of walking joint to joint.

The count is necessary but not sufficient — the solver checks the matrix, not the count.

Three misconceptions, and the move that kills each

“More members always makes it stronger.” A stiffer path attracts more force, so an added member can overload its neighbour — and every member costs money. Try it: add a member to a design that just made par and watch the peak move.
“The verticals do nothing, they're spacers.” In a Pratt they are in compression carrying panel shear to the top chord; in a Howe they are in tension. Try it: load a Pratt, X-ray on, erase one vertical — its panel becomes a rectangle and the truss becomes a mechanism.
“Tension and compression are just plus and minus.” The same 4 m member holds 300 kN pulled and 45 kN pushed; the sign selects a different failure mode. Try it: note a design's peak use, then rebuild it upside down under the deck.

Questions that land

  1. The bare rectangle folded without any member changing length. Where did the load go?
  2. Two designs, same cost and joints — one props the deck from above, one hangs it from below. Which is safer, and what in Pcr = π²EI/L² says so?
  3. A Warren's diagonals alternate. Take one bottom joint and use ΣFy = 0 to show why.
  4. Why is the bottom chord's force greatest at mid-span and near zero at the supports?
  5. One member snapped and the whole bridge came down. What does that say about spare load paths?
  6. Level 5's pier drops par sharply. Why does halving a span do much more than halve the forces?
  7. Turn on Self-weight and retest a design that just made par. Why does it matter more as spans grow?

How to play

You are the fabricator. Span the gap, get the vehicle across, and do it for less money than anyone else. The solver underneath is the real thing — if your bridge falls down, it falls down for a reason you can read off the screen.

Your first bridge in 60 seconds

  1. Draw the roadway: with Draw selected, drag between neighboring grid points from the left orange support to the right one.
  2. Make triangles: add joints above or below the road, then connect diagonal members. Rectangles fold; triangles hold their shape.
  3. Read the bridge: leave X-ray on. Red members are being pulled, blue members are being compressed, and brighter members are closer to failure.
  4. Run it: press Load test. If it fails, use the debrief to find the first weak member, return to the build, and change one thing.

Build

  1. Drag from one grid point to another to add a member. Joints appear automatically.
  2. You are billed the way a fabricator bills: steel by weight (9.4 kg per metre at $4.80/kg, so about $45 a metre) plus $180 for every joint you place — a gusset plate, bolts and someone to install them. Abutment bearings come with the level. Nothing longer than 4 m.
  3. Because connections cost real money, more panels is not free. Bracing a panel you already have adds steel only; adding a panel adds a joint too. That is why real trusses use as few panels as they can get away with.
  4. The orange anchors are fixed by the level and cannot be moved or deleted.
  5. The car needs an unbroken roadway along the deck line to drive on.
  6. Erase removes a member or joint. Undo is Ctrl+Z.

Understand

  1. X-ray parks the test vehicle at mid-span and colours every member by the force it carries — before you commit.
  2. Check a joint: tap any joint and every force meeting there is drawn as an arrow, with the running total printed. It is always zero — that is the whole of statics. (If the structure can still fold there is no equilibrium yet, and the panel will tell you so.)
  3. Numbers prints the force in kilonewtons on each member.

Test

The vehicle is solved at every position as it crosses. Members that exceed their rating snap, and the structure is immediately re-solved with what is left — which is how one failure becomes a collapse. Compression members fail far below tension members of the same size, and the longer they are, the weaker they get. Survive the crossing for the least money to beat par.
