How this works

Four acts · about 15 minutes
  1. Read the prediction. Ten of them, all real, all sourced. The speaker and the date are hidden.
  2. Guess two things. Drag the slider to the year you think it was said, then say whether it came true. Then press Lock it in and find out.
  3. Watch the split. Some of these are people promising AI was nearly here. Some are people saying it would never work. At the end you get scored on both separately, and that comparison is the whole point.

There is nothing to beat. Most rooms do well on one kind of prediction and badly on the other, and which one is which is usually a surprise.

The four acts
  • Guess the year — ten predictions, 1950 to 2024.
  • The two winters — a timeline you can scrub, laid out so the repeated pattern is visible rather than asserted.
  • Anatomy — the first winter, the second winter, and now, on the same five stages.
  • Now — what rhymes with the two winters, what is genuinely different, what was cut for lack of a source, and every source.
The two buttons, top right
  • ? — reopens this panel at any point.
  • ⚙ — presenter mode, the presenter’s notes, and a self-test that checks every claim against its declared source, live, in front of whoever is asking.

Nothing here is generated by an AI. It is a set of dated quotations, a timeline and a link to the original source of each one. The whole app is one file that runs offline and stores nothing.

Tap outside, or press Esc, to close.

Help

Proof

Checks that every claim on screen resolves to a declared source, that no source is cited and never used, that every source URL points at a specific page rather than a bare host, that the timeline runs forward in time, that each winter contains at least one funding withdrawal, that the counts quoted on the scorecard match the cards actually in the deck, and that the scoring refuses to mark an unresolved prediction. Results appear here and in the browser console.
