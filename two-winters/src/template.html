Where the winter dates are soft

Four funding examples, not a comparable total

There is no continuous, sourceable series of AI funding across these seventy-six years — particularly not for US spending before 1980 — so this demo does not draw one. These are four sourceable funding examples with different bases: programme budgets, programme expenditure, and a private funding round. They use three currencies, different price years, and no common accounting basis. Read them as dated evidence, not as a combined total or trend.

Compare the conditions; do not force a cycle.

Reading across a row compares a historical condition with another and with the present. Two cells in the present column remain open: no single binding constraint has been established, and no withdrawal has occurred. Those absences do not forecast what happens next; they keep the question honest.

What rhymes, and what does not.

Two columns, argued as honestly as the sources allow. Neither column wins. The point of having both is that you leave able to judge a specific claim on its specific evidence, rather than reaching for a mood.

Guide

How this works · about 15 minutes
  1. Read the prediction. Ten of them, all real, all sourced. The speaker and the date are hidden.
  2. Guess two things. Drag the slider to the year you think it was said, then say whether it came true. Then press Lock it in and find out.
  3. Watch the split. Some of these are people promising AI was nearly here. Some are people saying it would never work. At the end you get scored on both separately, and that comparison is the whole point.

There is nothing to beat. Most rooms do well on one kind of prediction and badly on the other, and which one is which is usually a surprise.

The four acts
  • Guess the year — ten curated claims, 1950 to 2024.
  • The two winters — a timeline you can scrub, using five lenses to compare different historical pathways.
  • Anatomy — compare the first winter, the second winter, and now without treating them as one cycle.
  • Now — what rhymes with the two winters, what is genuinely different, what was cut for lack of a source, and every source.
The two buttons, top right
  • ? — Guide. Reopens this panel at any point.
  • ⚙ — Settings: Open Presenter Notes, Presentation mode, Reset, and a self-test that checks every claim against its declared source, live, in front of whoever is asking.

Nothing here is generated by an AI. It is a set of dated quotations, a timeline and a link to the original source of each one. The whole app is one file that runs offline and stores nothing.

Settings

Presentation · Session · Proof
Presentation

The session plan: what to say at each act, which predictions to keep if you are short of time, the questions you will get, and a six-minute cut. This is the printable guide itself, not a summary of it — the same file that ships beside this one, shown on screen.

Oversized UI for a projector: larger controls and a taller timeline.

Session

Back to a fresh load, without reloading: every answer cleared, Act I back at its opening screen, the timeline rewound to the full span, the self-test output cleared. Presentation mode is a projector setting rather than demo state, so Reset leaves it as you set it.

Proof

Checks that every claim on screen resolves to a declared source, that no source is cited and never used, that every source URL points at a specific page rather than a bare host, that the timeline runs forward in time, that each winter contains at least one funding withdrawal, that the counts quoted on the scorecard match the cards actually in the deck, and that the scoring refuses to mark an unresolved prediction. Results appear here and in the browser console.
