How this works · about 15 minutes
  1. Read the claim. Ten sourced claims, with the speaker and date hidden: five time-bounded forecasts, four assessments or untimed ambitions, and one warning.
  2. Guess the year, then classify. Drag the slider to the year you think it was said. For a time-bounded forecast, call its outcome; for an assessment or untimed ambition, identify the kind of claim. Then press Lock it in and find out.
  3. Watch the split. Forecasts receive an outcome score only when they name a time window. Assessments and untimed ambitions stay unscored context, because later events do not turn them into failed prophecies.

AI Winters: Boom and Bust — presenter guide

Part two of “AI: From Zero to Takeoff”. Runs 12–18 minutes full, 6 minutes cut. One HTML file, offline, no AI at runtime.

Why this demo exists

It follows Takeoff, which ends with the room considering AI gains alongside limits and measurement conditions. The honest next question — and you will get it, usually from someone who has been in a technical field a long time — is “is this another bubble?”

This demo answers that with evidence instead of reassurance. AI has contracted twice, through different combinations of technical limits, institutional choices, and markets. The histories rhyme; they do not provide a fixed sequence for the present.

The one sentence to land: a prediction needs a deadline and an outcome; an assessment needs evidence about its own moment. Do not grade a historical claim until you know which kind it is.

Act I — running the prediction round

Do the first two cards yourself, out loud, so the room sees the mechanic. Then hand it over — ask for a show of hands on the year before you drag the slider, and ask for a verbal verdict or classification before you press Lock it in. For the four context cards, choose Assessment or untimed ambition when the claim does not supply a scoreable future window. The room being wrong together is the experience; a silent room reading a screen is not.

Short of time?

Keep four: Simon & Newell 1957 (over-promise, right destination), ALPAC 1966 (assessment versus forecast), Schank & Minsky 1984 (a prediction that was right), and Amodei 2024 (still open). Those four carry the whole argument. Skip to Act III and close.

What the scorecard is for

It scores time-bounded forecasts and displays dated assessments without grading their future accuracy. That distinction is the teaching point: a claim about conditions in 1966 needs different evidence from a claim about what will happen by 1976.

“Before calling a historical claim right or wrong, ask whether it actually made a prediction. If it did, name the outcome and deadline. If it did not, assess its own evidence.”

Act II — the timeline

Do not narrate a cycle into existence. Drag the slider slowly from 1950 and use the rows as comparison lenses: claims, institutional bets, constraints, reassessment, withdrawal. Ask which causes matter in each episode and which do not carry across.

  • The ‘ and ’ buttons step one event at a time if you want to stop and read one.
  • Tap any dot for the full text and its source.
  • Point at the current entries as evidence about today, not a third historical run.

Do not let the lenses turn into a prophecy. The correct line is: “these comparisons tell us what to investigate. They do not establish that a withdrawal will occur.”

The funding examples — read the caveat aloud

They use three currencies and different price years. No continuous, sourceable funding series exists across these years, so the demo shows dated evidence rather than drawing a combined total or a line it cannot defend.

Act III — compare

Choose one of the five lens buttons; its navy fill marks the selected lens. Compare the three era headings, then open each era’s Details for the full explanation and named sources. Escape closes the side panel and returns focus to its button. Compare causes, not step numbers. Two cells in the “Today” column remain open: no single binding technical constraint is established here, and this snapshot does not establish a comparable broad contraction.

“Hindsight makes old constraints easier to see. It does not prove that today has the same constraint or that it must end the same way. Ask what evidence would support the claim.”

Act IV — today

Expand a similarity and a difference, giving both columns roughly equal time. The supporting statements start closed so the room can investigate one claim at a time. The strongest item on each side:

  • Rhymes: in February 2026 OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified after contamination concerns and an audit of 138 selected hard tasks found faulty tests in 59.4% of that subset. That is a warning about one evaluation, not a field verdict.
  • Different: scaling behaviour measured across more than seven orders of magnitude, and reported consumer reach at a far larger scale. Earlier booms also had deployed systems and paying organisations, so be precise about the difference.

Close on the checklist, not on a verdict: given any AI claim, ask whether it is a forecast or an assessment, what evidence supports it, and what evidence would move it.

Questions you will get

“So is it a bubble or not?”

Refuse the binary, in one sentence, then give the useful answer: current funding, reported usage, technical reliability, and returns are distinct questions. Earlier eras also had deployed systems and organisational users, though current consumer reach is much larger. A financial correction and a technological dead end are different claims and need different evidence.

“Aren’t the winter dates made up?”

Partly, and the demo says so. The bands are conventions for broader contractions, not dates stamped by a single event. The 1987 hardware collapse is a useful second-winter anchor; the first has more distributed causes and withdrawals, including but not limited to ALPAC. The ends are also conventional and historians disagree; that is why the bands have soft edges and explanatory notes.

“Where did you get this?”

Open Settings → Run the self-test in front of them. It re-derives, live, that every claim resolves to a declared source, that no source is cited and never used, and that the counts quoted on screen match the data. Then scroll to Act IV, which lists every source under Every source and, separately, everything researched and cut for lack of one. Both supporting sections open on request.

“Why is that quote from a quotation-checking website?”

Because the Simon and Minsky lines are routed through a quotation-checking source that cites printed books and page references. This build has not inspected those original pages, so it labels the claims reported rather than primary. That distinction is part of the lesson: a plausible citation route is not the same as direct verification.

Before you present

  • Turn on Settings → Presentation mode for projector-sized type.
  • Run the self-test once so you know it is green before anyone asks.
  • Between rooms, Settings → Reset returns the whole demo to a fresh load — answers cleared, Act I back at its opening screen, timeline rewound — without a reload. It leaves Presentation mode on, so the projector does not change under you.

  • No network is needed at any point. If the room’s wifi is down, nothing changes.
