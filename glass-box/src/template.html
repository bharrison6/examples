A language model is a guess-the-next-piece machine

Underneath every chatbot is a single trick: given some text, predict what comes next — over and over, one piece at a time. This page carries a complete, working language model with 42,458 parameters. It is about fifty million times smaller than a frontier model, but it is the same kind of machine: same layers, same attention, same training rule. You are going to watch it learn to write, from nothing, on this device.

It starts untrained — random numbers, no knowledge of English, no knowledge of anything. Nothing here is faked or downloaded: every number you will see is computed in front of you.

1.1 First, text becomes tokens

A model cannot read letters. Text is chopped into tokens and each token gets a number. Our little model uses one token per character. Type anything:

At frontier scale: real models chop text into word-pieces instead of characters — unbelievable might become un believ able — with a vocabulary of 50,000–200,000 pieces instead of our 26 characters. The idea is identical: text in, numbers in a list out.

1.2 The machine itself: parameters in layers

The model is a stack of arithmetic. Numbers flow in, get multiplied by big tables of learned numbers — the parameters, also called weights — and a prediction falls out the far end. This is the actual layout of the model on this page. Tap a row to see the real numbers it holds right now:

Two kinds of layer alternate, and they split the work: attention layers let each position look back at earlier tokens and pull in the relevant ones, and feed-forward layers transform what attention gathered. Stack that pair a few times — our model does it twice, frontier models do it around a hundred times — and that is the whole architecture.

Parameter counts of well-known models, on a logarithmic scale — each full bar grows by digits, not by size — GPT-2 holds 2,900× this page’s parameters and a frontier model 40 million×. Frontier models are the same recipe, scaled up and trained on trillions of tokens instead of one children's story.

1.3 Training: watch it learn to write

Training shows the model hundreds of thousands of tiny quizzes — here are 40 characters of a story; guess character 41 — and after each guess, nudges all 42,458 parameters a hair in the direction that would have made the right answer more likely. That nudge rule (gradient descent) is the entire teacher. Its textbook is one small original story about a robot in a garden, and it starts from pure static:

Loss is how wrong its guesses are, on average (lower is better). Watching this number fall is watching it learn — there is no other teacher anywhere in the code.

1.4 Prediction: the only thing it ever does

Give the trained model some text and it produces one thing: a probability for every token in its vocabulary to come next. Chat, essays, code — all of it is this table, sampled one token at a time, fed back in, repeated. Try it (and try switching eras to see the same machine with different numbers inside):

era 
temperature 0.8

Temperature rescales the dice before rolling. Near 0 it always takes the top guess — safe, repetitive. Higher, and it takes risks — creative, then unhinged. This is the same knob you see in every AI API.

1.5 Attention: reading the model's mind

Inside each attention layer, every position computes how much to look at every earlier token. These weights are not drawn for show — they are read out of the live model on this page. Tap any character to see where the model looks while predicting the next one; gold means looking hard.

The full map: each row is a position, each column an earlier position it may attend to. The lower-left triangle shape is the rule that you cannot look at the future. Different heads learn different habits — one may track the previous word, another hunts for a repeated word far back. Attention is what lets "it" find what "it" refers to — and it is why models handle long, winding sentences at all.

What is real here: everything. A hand-written transformer — embeddings, two attention-plus-feed-forward blocks, layer norm, Adam optimizer — training and running in plain JavaScript on this device. No network, no AI service, nothing pre-trained. What frontier models add is scale: more layers, wider layers, vastly more text — and after that, the training in Acts 2 and 3.

A predictor imitates. A reasoner works things out.

Act 1's model has a ceiling: it answers in one reflex guess. Ask a hard question and there is nowhere for intermediate work to happen. Reasoning models are ordinary language models given two extra things: they are trained to write out their working before answering, and the attempts that check out are the ones reinforced. That is the recipe behind the current wave of "thinking" models.

To see it without hand-waving, we shrink the problem to something a phone can train: 3-digit addition. Hard enough that a one-reflex guess fails; simple enough that everything is checkable.

2.1 One brain, two educations

Two copies of the same tiny transformer — same width, same depth, same step budget of wall-clock time. One is shown only problem → answer. The other is shown the written-out column arithmetic between them. Then both sit the same 100-question exam of unseen problems:

Trains Model D, then Model S, then examines both — about two to three minutes total on a recent phone. Accuracy checkpoints appear as each model trains.

Why this matters: the chain of thought gives the same small network somewhere to put intermediate results. Each little step is easy to learn; the direct leap is not. "Thinking" in modern AI is exactly this — extra tokens the model writes to itself before committing to an answer.

2.3 Thinking longer: test-time compute

Once the work is written down, it can be checked. We deliberately use an under-trained sibling of Model S — same recipe, stopped at 400 steps (— on its own) — let it try each problem several times with the dice turned up, and keep only attempts whose written steps a small verifier certifies — every column sum true, every carry consistent, the final answer assembled from the steps. The verifier is never told the true answer. More attempts, more accuracy — paid for in compute, not in training:

Train the models in §2.1 first.

This is the "thinking time" dial on modern reasoning models: sample more chains, check them, keep the good ones. Accuracy climbs with no new training at all.

2.4 Teaching itself: reinforcement

Now close the loop. The same half-trained model poses itself fresh problems, tries each a few times, and the attempts its verifier certifies as correct become new training data. Nothing else changes — it studies its own best work. This is reinforcement learning in miniature: the verifier is the reward signal, and it is the essence of how reasoning models are made.

Train the models in §2.1 first. Each round: ~120 self-posed problems, up to 3 attempts each, then fine-tune on the kept work. Press it three or four times.

What is real here: both models genuinely train on this device, the exam problems are genuinely unseen, the verifier genuinely checks arithmetic, and the self-improvement rounds genuinely reuse the model's own certified chains. What frontier labs add: the graded work is math, code, science and dialogue instead of column addition; the verifier may be unit tests, proof checkers, or humans comparing answers (RLHF); and the loop runs on thousands of GPUs. The shape of the idea is the one on this page.

A reasoner that can only talk. An agent can do.

Act 2's model still lives in a sealed box: text in, text out. An agent is a reasoning model given tools — search, calculators, code, files — and put in a loop: the model writes an action, the tool really runs, the result is appended to the conversation, and the model reads it and acts again. The model never stops being a next-token predictor. It is just predicting in a loop whose text has consequences.

3.3 What the loop teaches

    
Errors are survivable. Watch the calculator turn — the agent writes
    9 x $13.00, the tool genuinely rejects it, and the error message lands in
    the context like any other observation. The next prediction reads the error and corrects. Agents do
    not need to be perfect; they need to see what happened.

    
The context window is the agent's entire memory. The model itself is frozen —
    nothing is written to its parameters between turns. Re-run the task with the tiny window
    setting: once the goal scrolls out of view, this agent — honestly wired to see only that window —
    stops dead, because it no longer knows what it is doing. Every real agent system is a fight against
    exactly this.

    
Tools are powers, and powers are scoped. Our agent can read five documents and
    do arithmetic — nothing else. Real agents are given real powers (your files, your browser, your
    terminal), which is why serious systems run them in sandboxes, ask before irreversible actions, and
    log every tool call. The loop you just watched is also the audit trail.

    
What is real here: the tools are real — the calculator genuinely parses
    and errors, search genuinely searches, the loop and the context genuinely work as shown, and the
    decision function genuinely sees only the visible window (that is why the tiny-window run breaks).
    The one scripted part is the decision itself: a hand-written rulebook plays the role of Act 2's
    reasoning model, because a model good enough to plan this runs in a data center, not a phone. Wire
    Act 2's idea to Act 3's loop at scale and you have Claude, ChatGPT's agents, coding assistants — all
    of it.

Misconceptions to draw out

    
“It's understanding the story.” It is predicting the next character of one children's
      story. Whether understanding at frontier scale differs in kind or only in degree is the question to raise, not
      resolve — the demo gives both sides ammunition.

    
“It memorized the text.” 42,458 parameters cannot hold 4,700 characters verbatim plus
      grammar, and its samples are recombinations, not quotes. (Frontier models can memorize — different regime.)

    
“Reasoning models are just prompted to think step by step.” Prompting only elicits it.
      §2.1 puts the steps in the training data; §2.4 reinforces chains that verify.

    
“The agent wanted the right answer.” The decision rules here are scripted and the page says
      so. What is genuinely shown: the loop, real tools, real errors, and total dependence on context.

    
“Bigger context windows will fix agents.” They help, and real windows are ~200k+ tokens.
      But cost grows with context, attention gets diluted, and tasks can outlive any window — hence note-taking and retrieval.

    
“Too small to say anything about ChatGPT or Claude.” What transfers is the mechanism — same
      architecture, same training rule, same loop — not the capability. Every “What is real here” box says where the
      toy ends.


    
Questions you will get

    
    
      
  • How long would a frontier model take to train this way? This page does ~15 updates/s on ~43k
      parameters; frontier training is ~10²⁵ operations — months on tens of thousands of GPUs. Same rule, different budget.
    • 
      
  • Where do the words come from at temperature 0? Always the top of the probability table. All variety in
      AI output is controlled dice-rolling over that table.
    • 
      
  • Is the verifier cheating? It never sees the true answer — only whether the written steps are valid
      arithmetic that assembles into the stated answer. That is why it can reject a right answer with wrong work.
    • 
      
  • What would make Act 3's agent real? Replace the scripted rulebook with Act 2's model at scale.
