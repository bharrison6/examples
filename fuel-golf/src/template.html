A burn moves the far side of your orbit

You are at the top of an ellipse and you push forward. Which part of the orbit moves?

Before you start. Periapsis is the low, fast point of an orbit; apoapsis is the high, slow point. Δv ("delta-vee") is the speed change a burn buys, in metres per second, and it is the whole fuel budget: a tank holds so many m/s and not one more. This is the first demo of Part four, so it carries no handover from Demo 6 — it is an AI-built classroom tool, and its engine is a real gravity integrator rather than a script.

Demo 7 of 16 · Part four · Physics · Stage 2

The same Δv, spent where you are fastest

Escaping Earth needs a fixed amount of energy — so does it cost the same Δv wherever you burn?

Before you start. Specific orbital energy is ε = ½v² − μ/r, and escaping means getting ε above zero; a very tall ellipse still falls back. A burn adds Δv to your speed, so the kinetic half goes from ½v² to ½(v+Δv)² — a gain of v·Δv + ½Δv². The v in that first term is your speed at the instant you burn. Stage 1 showed where a burn acts; this stage is about what it costs.

Level 3 starts on an ellipse with periapsis 11,100 km and apoapsis 70,100 km above the surface. Which burn point escapes for less Δv?

Takeaway

The same Δv buys energy in proportion to how fast you already are, so escape is cheapest at periapsis: 661 m/s here against 1,835 m/s from apoapsis. That is the Oberth effect.

A real engine takes time

Your engine needs hours to deliver its Δv. Where in the orbit should that burn happen?

Before you start. Stages 1 and 2 assumed an impulsive burn: the whole Δv arriving in one instant, at one speed. A real engine has a fixed acceleration — 0.055 m/s² here, then 0.013 — so the Δv is smeared over an arc of the orbit, and only the part delivered while you are fast earns the full premium stage 2 measured.

Predict

The full burn lasts a noticeable arc. Light it exactly at periapsis, or start before periapsis?

Try

Read Time to periapsis in the HUD and Burn duration in the planner, type a lead in minutes beside → Pe, then commit.

Takeaway

Spread over time, a burn is cheapest centred on periapsis — 775 m/s centred against 919 m/s lit at periapsis — and with a weak engine six short kicks cost 731 m/s where one long burn costs 1,498.

One question across four stages: why does the same burn buy more energy in one place than another?

Where this sits: the first AI-built classroom tool in Part four. Its goal is to make the Oberth effect something you measure rather than something you are told, and its assumptions are one tap away in Details.

  1. Burn at apoapsis. Commit a prediction, press → Ap, then 🔥 Plan burn and watch the dotted preview as you drag Δv.
  2. Escape at periapsis. Commit the prediction to unlock Level 3, press → Pe, and burn until the ORBIT chip says ESCAPING.
  3. Finite thrust. Unlocks once you have escaped. Use the HUD's Time to periapsis and the lead box to start the burn early.
  4. Sandbox. No goal, deep tank, every engine. Outside the path.

What is live

What this page actually computes

Live the flight view and the HUD

Every position, speed, altitude and energy on screen is integrated in your browser as you watch. Nothing on the map is a recording or a scripted animation: the Oberth advantage is not written into the game, it falls out of the integrator.

Sourced Earth's μ and radius

μ = 398,600 km³/s² and radius 6,371 km, so every number transfers to a real mission. NASA's Earth fact sheet prints GM as 0.39860 × 10&sup6; km³/s² and the volumetric mean radius as 6371.000 km.

Measureddeterministic — no seed the seven pars

Each par is a Δv budget a skilled pilot can beat, found by running this same physics offline. Level 1's ideal circularization measures 604 m/s against a par of 712. The integrator has no random element, so re-running test-physics.js reproduces every figure exactly.

How it works

ΔKE = v·Δv + ½Δv²  ·  ε = ½v² − μ/r

Gravity is planet-centred and Newtonian, integrated with velocity Verlet at a fixed substep — a symplectic scheme, which is why the orbit does not spiral in from numerical drift: measured energy drift over fifty orbits is 9 × 10⁻¹⁴. The orbit drawn as a solid line is the integrated path; the dotted preview is the same integrator run forward from the burn you are planning.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Two bodies, plus at most one moon. No sun, no other planets, no J₂ oblateness, no third-body perturbations.
  • No atmosphere and no drag. A periapsis just above the surface is free here and would not be in reality.
  • Burns in stages 1 and 2 are impulsive — the whole Δv at one instant. Stage 3 removes that assumption; nothing else does.
  • Δv is the only cost. There is no mass, no specific impulse and no rocket equation, so the tank is a Δv budget rather than kilograms of propellant. That is the standard mission-planning currency, but it hides the fact that a real ship gets lighter as it burns.
  • The craft is a point mass with no orientation, so pointing the engine is free and instant.

Sources

  • NASA NSSDC, Earth Fact Sheet — read 2026-09-16: "GM (x 106 km3/s2) 0.39860" and "Volumetric mean radius (km) 6371.000", the two constants this simulation uses.
Boundary: this is not a mission planner. It teaches one relationship — energy gained per unit Δv scales with current speed — and it deliberately strips away everything that would otherwise compete for attention. Do not use its numbers to plan anything that flies.

Live the escape test

"Escaping" is checked against the integrated state each frame: specific orbital energy ε above zero and a radius past the system edge. A tall ellipse that crosses the edge with ε still negative is refused, and the page says why.

Measureddeterministic — no seed the 661 / 1,835 m/s comparison

Both figures are the minimum prograde Δv that escapes from Level 3's orbit, found by bisection against this exact engine: 661 m/s from periapsis, 1,835 m/s from apoapsis, a ratio of 2.77. The simulated periapsis figure also matches the closed-form vesc − vp to three figures, which is the check that says the integrator and the theory agree.

Illustrative Level 5's moon

Invented, and the map says so beside it. Its orbit radius, mass parameter and size were chosen so that a useful gravity assist fits inside a class period; our own Moon is both further out and lighter relative to this setup. Its gravity is then integrated for real, including the indirect term (the moon pulls Earth too, so the planet-centred frame is not inertial).

How it works

Δε = v·Δv + ½Δv²

A burn changes only the kinetic term: the potential −μ/r is unchanged at the instant of an impulsive burn, because you have not moved. So the energy a given Δv buys is set by v, and v peaks at periapsis. The HUD's "energy gained per 1 Δv" readout is therefore numerically just your current speed — a claim the test suite verifies numerically rather than asserting.

The usual next question is where the extra energy comes from. The propellant's chemical energy is fixed; what changes is how it is divided between ship and exhaust. At high speed the engine acts over more distance per second, so more of that energy ends up in the ship and less in the plume left behind.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The burn is impulsive — instantaneous, at one speed. Real burns are not, which is exactly what stage 3 is for.
  • The system edge is a chosen radius, not a physical boundary. It stands in for "far enough that Earth no longer dominates", and each level sets its own.
  • The gravity assist is a restricted three-body problem: the moon moves on a fixed circular orbit and the craft never affects it. A real assist perturbs the assisting body, imperceptibly.
  • Steering is free. Pointing the ship costs no fuel and no time.

Sources

  • NASA NSSDC, Earth Fact Sheet — read 2026-09-16: GM 0.39860 × 106 km3/s2, volumetric mean radius 6371.000 km.
Boundary: the 2.77× gap is a property of this orbit, not a universal constant. Make the ellipse rounder and the gap shrinks; on a circular orbit there is no periapsis to prefer. What generalises is the direction of the effect, not the size of it.

Live the powered arc

A finite-thrust burn is integrated with RK4 while the engine is lit, then the coast returns to velocity Verlet. The planner's burn duration is Δv divided by the engine's acceleration, and the percentage beside it is that duration against your current orbital period — both read off the live state.

Measureddeterministic — no seed775 / 919 and 731 / 1,498 m/s

On Level 6's 0.055 m/s² engine, escape costs 775 m/s with the burn centred on periapsis and 919 m/s lit at periapsis. On Level 7's 0.013 m/s² engine, six kicks of roughly 2.8 hours each cost 731 m/s where one long centred burn costs 1,498. All four are minima found against this engine offline.

Sourcedthe practice — ESA's SMART-1 mission page, 2006; the motive is reasoned from this page's measurement and MIT 16.07 L17 low-thrust stages escape by repeated perigee burns

A flown mission did exactly this. ESA's SMART-1 spent its Earth-escape cruise, in the operator's words, with "thrust arcs around the perigee and coast arcs around the apogee", raising apogee a little on every pass until the Moon captured it — the practice, stated by the people who flew it. Why it works is not stated on that page; it is what this page measures (731 against 1,498) and what MIT's 16.07 lecture notes derive: for a given Δv the energy gain is largest where the speed is largest, so a burn too long for one perigee pass is split across several. Present the practice as sourced and the motive as reasoning.

How it works

tburn = Δv / a  ·  centre the arc on periapsis

Integrate v·Δv over the burn rather than evaluating it once. Every second of thrust is delivered at whatever speed you happen to have then, so the cheapest single burn is the one whose arc is symmetric about the speed peak. Light it at the peak and the entire second half happens on the way back out, at falling speed. When the burn is longer than roughly a third of the orbit, no placement saves it and the answer is to split it.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Steering losses are ignored. The thrust is applied along the chosen direction for the whole burn with no cosine loss from the velocity vector rotating underneath it. This is the largest simplification in the stage and it makes finite thrust look slightly cheaper than it is.
  • Thrust and acceleration are constant. No throttling, no mass loss, so no acceleration ramp as the tank empties.
  • The engine lights and cuts instantly, with no ignition transient.
  • Time‑warp is capped at 10× while thrusting, so a long burn is integrated finely enough to be honest rather than fast.

Sources

  • NASA NSSDC, Earth Fact Sheet — read 2026-09-16: the μ and radius the powered arcs are integrated against.
  • ESA, "Ion engine gets SMART-1 to the Moon" (31 August 2006) — read 2026-09-16: "thrust arcs around the perigee and coast arcs around the apogee"; the lunar braking manoeuvre "was used around perilune".
  • MIT OCW 16.07 Dynamics, Lecture L17, Orbit Transfers (Widnall & Peraire, 2008) — read 2026-09-16: "for a given Δv, the change in energy will be largest when … vi is maximum", with the perigee-escape example; also the impulsive assumption "when the burn time of the rocket is much smaller than the period of the orbit" that this stage relaxes.
Boundary: with steering losses out and the rocket equation out, this stage shows the shape of the finite-thrust penalty, not its size. Treat 775-against-919 as "centring matters and here is roughly how much", not as a figure to carry into a real Δv budget.

Live the whole Sandbox, and the debrief's arithmetic

Same integrator, same units, no goal and no par. The debrief's show the math panel is not a canned explanation: it re-evaluates v·Δv + ½Δv² on the burns you actually committed and compares the total against the energy the simulation ended up with, so a discrepancy would show.

Illustrative the Sandbox's moon and its 200-unit tank

Both invented for free play. The tank is deep so that an experiment is never cut short by fuel, and the moon is a second fictional body on a different orbit from Level 5's. Neither is meant to correspond to anything.

How it works

Δεtotal = Σ (v·Δv + ½Δv²)  +  whatever the moon lent you

Coasting never changes ε, because gravity is conservative. So the energy ledger has exactly two kinds of entry: burns, which you paid for, and moon encounters, which you did not. The debrief prints them as a running total, which is what makes an assist visible as a line item rather than as magic.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Nothing here is scored or saved. The Sandbox is outside the lesson path and has no par, so the leaderboard ignores it.
  • Every simplification from the other three stages still applies — no atmosphere, no rocket equation, point mass, free steering.
  • The engine choices are the same three the lesson stages use, offered together so they can be compared on one orbit rather than across levels.

Sources

  • NASA NSSDC, Earth Fact Sheet — read 2026-09-16: the μ and radius the Sandbox integrates against, unchanged from the lesson stages.
Boundary: a cheap escape you find here is cheap in this model. The model has no drag, no mass loss and no steering cost, so a strategy that exploits those absences — skimming the surface, or turning the ship instantly — is telling you about the simulation rather than about spaceflight.
