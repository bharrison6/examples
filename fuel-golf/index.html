⛳ Orbital Mechanics Golf — Teacher Guide

Fuel Golf: orbital mechanics & the Oberth effect, taught by a fuel gauge

What students do

Each mission gives a spacecraft, a goal (circularize, raise an orbit, escape, transfer, moon flyby) and a par Δv budget. Real Newtonian gravity is integrated live, and the planet is Earth at true scale (μ = 398,600 km³/s², R = 6,371 km): speeds in km/s, Δv in m/s, altitudes in km — every number transfers to real missions. Students time-warp, aim a burn, preview the dotted trajectory, commit. Lowest total Δv wins. Runs offline in any browser — open index.html (Chromebook-friendly). The moon is fictional (closer/heavier than Luna) so an assist fits one class.

20-minute session plan

  • 0–3 minProject the game (⚙ Settings → Presentation mode). The Guide overlay opens on first load and explains the controls; dismiss it with Fly and reopen it any time from ❓ Guide. Fly Level 1 together: show time-warp, the burn planner, and the dotted preview. Vocabulary: periapsis (low, fast), apoapsis (high, slow), prograde.
  • 3–8 minStudents play Levels 1–2 in pairs. Ask them to open the Physics HUD and watch “energy gained per 1 Δv” as the ship swings around.
  • 8–15 minThe main event: Level 3, “Escape Artist.” Par is 791 m/s, but the tank holds 2,057 — escaping Earth from apoapsis costs ≈1,835 m/s, while the same escape from periapsis costs ≈661. Let them discover this. When someone makes par, have them explain where they burned and what the HUD showed.
  • 15–19 minDebrief on the projector using a student’s energy-vs-time plot and the “show the math” panel below it, which evaluates Δε = v·Δv + ½Δv² with that student’s actual numbers and checks it against the simulation. The burn is a vertical jump in ε — taller when v was larger, for the same Δv.
  • 19–20 minPost the leaderboard. Levels 4–7 (Hohmann, powered flyby, finite thrust) make excellent homework or a second session.

The physics — Oberth effect

An impulsive burn changes speed by Δv along the velocity. Kinetic energy per unit mass goes from ½v² to ½(v+Δv)², so

ΔKE = v·Δv + ½Δv²

The v·Δv term dominates: the same Δv buys energy in proportion to how fast you are already moving. On an orbit, speed peaks at periapsis — so a burn there buys the most orbital energy ε = ½v² − μ/r. Gravitational potential is unchanged at the instant of the burn; only the KE jump differs. In the game, the HUD’s “energy per unit Δv” readout is exactly v, and students can verify the ≈2.8× escape-cost gap on Level 3 themselves.

Where does the extra energy come from? Rocket fuel’s energy is fixed, but the useful energy delivered to the ship is force × distance. At high speed the engine acts over a longer distance per second, so more of the propellant’s energy ends up in the ship and less in the exhaust left behind.

Misconception 1: “The burn is better at periapsis because you’re closer to the planet.” Proximity is not the mechanism — speed is. ΔKE = v·Δv + ½Δv² contains v, not r. Being deep in the well matters only because falling there is what made the ship fast. A ship moving equally fast far from the planet would gain just as much energy from the same burn.
Misconception 2: “The Oberth effect is the same as a gravity assist.” An unpowered gravity assist (Level 5’s moon) steals energy from the moon’s orbital motion — no fuel needed, and the moon slows imperceptibly. An Oberth burn spends fuel and merely spends it at the most profitable moment. A “powered flyby” combines both.
Misconception 3: “Δv is like distance — it costs the same anywhere.” Δv spent is the same anywhere; the energy it buys is not. That is the whole game.

Finite thrust (levels 6–7)

Levels 1–5 use impulsive burns; levels 6–7 give the engine limited acceleration (0.055 and 0.013 m/s²), so Δv is delivered over hours of arc (steering losses ignored). Two lessons emerge: a single burn is cheapest centered on periapsis — start early, or the late half happens at lower speed (lit at Pe: 919 m/s to escape; centered: 775) — and with the weak engine one long burn costs 1,498 m/s while six ~2.8 h perigee kicks cost 731. This is why real upper stages escape via repeated perigee kicks.

Discussion questions

  1. Level 3’s energy plot shows a taller jump in ε for the same Δv at periapsis. Which term of ΔKE = v·Δv + ½Δv² explains this?
  2. The HUD says “energy per unit Δv = current speed.” Differentiate ½v² to show why.
  3. Why does a prograde burn at periapsis raise the apoapsis, on the far side of the orbit?
  4. In Level 5, where does the escape energy come from when you fly close behind the moon without burning? What slows down in exchange?
  5. Real missions (e.g., Cassini’s Saturn orbit insertion, Juno’s perijove burns) plan burns at closest approach. Which levels model this, and why does NASA care so much about Δv budgets?
  6. Could you escape with a radial-out burn? Try it. Why does pushing “straight up” waste fuel?

Classroom logistics

  • ⚙ Settings holds the presenter controls: Open Presenter Notes (this page, on screen), Presentation mode (large UI for the back row, plus a 🎤 Notes button in the top bar) and Reset (the whole demo back to a first run — Level 1, scores, saved name and badges cleared; Presentation mode stays as you set it). Narrower controls sit alongside them: Clear scores and Clear badges.
  • Leaderboards persist per level on each machine; clear them between class periods with ⚙ Settings → Clear scores, or reset everything with ⚙ Settings → Reset.
  • Presenter Notes (⚙ Settings → Open Presenter Notes, or press N while presenting) show this exact page on screen — same words, one source, nothing fetched.
  • Pars (m/s): L1 712 · L2 1,107 · L3 791 · L4 1,819 · L5 1,424 · L6 838 · L7 823. Verified minimums: L3 escape ≈ 661 m/s from periapsis vs ≈ 1,835 m/s from apoapsis.
  • Keys: Space pause · B burn · Enter commit · Z undo · X cut engine · P/A warp to Pe/Ap · H HUD · M briefing · R restart level · ? Guide · N Presenter Notes. Undo rewinds the last burn (also offered after a crash) — students should iterate, not restart.
  • For L6–L7, point students at the HUD time-to-periapsis, the planner's burn duration, and the lead box beside → Pe: those three let them compute an ignition point instead of guessing.

Guide — how to play Orbital Mechanics Golf

You have a spacecraft, a tank of Δv, and a mission. Lowest Δv wins.

Your first two minutes

1. Read the mission — every level names a target and a par Δv budget, the fuel a skilled pilot needs. Your tank is bigger than par, so you can afford to experiment. 2. Time-warp to a good spot (→ Pe or → Ap). 3. Hit 🔥 Plan burn, aim, drag the Δv slider, and read the dotted preview before you spend anything. 4. Commit. Wrong call? ↶ Undo puts the fuel back. Nothing is ever wasted by trying.

Tap anywhere outside this box, press Esc, or hit Fly to close it — ❓ in the top bar brings it back any time.

Flying

Time-warp with ⏸ / 1× / 10× / 50× / 200×, or jump straight to periapsis (→ Pe, the low fast point) or apoapsis (→ Ap, the high slow point). Set a lead in minutes to stop short of the apsis — essential once your engine takes real time to burn. Drag the map to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom, ⊕ Follow keeps the camera on your craft.

Burning

Hit 🔥 Plan burn (or B). Pick a direction — prograde, retrograde, radial, or free angle (drag the map to aim) — and set Δv. The dotted line previews your new orbit, with hollow Pe/Ap markers showing where the new apsides land, before you spend fuel. Misjudged it? ↶ Undo rewinds the last burn.

The lesson hiding in the game

Adding Δv changes your kinetic energy by ΔKE = v·Δv + ½Δv². The v·Δv term means the same burn buys more energy when you're already moving fast — and you're fastest at periapsis. That's the Oberth effect. Open the Physics HUD and watch "energy per unit Δv" climb as you fall toward periapsis.

Real numbers

The planet is Earth at true scale: μ = 398,600 km³/s², radius 6,371 km. Speeds are km/s, Δv budgets m/s, altitudes km, and orbital periods run hours to days — which is why time-warp exists. The moon is fictional (closer and heavier than our Moon) so a gravity assist fits a class period.
