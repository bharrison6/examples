The same Δv, spent where you are fastest

Escaping Earth needs a fixed amount of energy — so does it cost the same Δv wherever you burn?

Before you start. Specific orbital energy is ε = ½v² − μ/r, and escaping means getting ε above zero; a very tall ellipse still falls back. A burn adds Δv to your speed, so the kinetic half goes from ½v² to ½(v+Δv)² — a gain of v·Δv + ½Δv². The v in that first term is your speed at the instant you burn. Stage 1 showed where a burn acts; this stage is about what it costs.

A real engine takes time

Your engine needs hours to deliver its Δv. Where in the orbit should that burn happen?

Before you start. Stages 1 and 2 assumed an impulsive burn: the whole Δv arriving in one instant, at one speed. A real engine has a fixed acceleration — 0.055 m/s² here, then 0.013 — so the Δv is smeared over an arc of the orbit, and only the part delivered while you are fast earns the full premium stage 2 measured.

Live the flight view and the HUD

Every position, speed, altitude and energy on screen is integrated in your browser as you watch. Nothing on the map is a recording or a scripted animation: the Oberth advantage is not written into the game, it falls out of the integrator.

Sourced Earth's μ and radius

μ = 398,600 km³/s² and radius 6,371 km, so every number transfers to a real mission. NASA's Earth fact sheet prints GM as 0.39860 × 10&sup6; km³/s² and the volumetric mean radius as 6371.000 km.

Measureddeterministic — no seed the seven pars

Each par is a Δv budget a skilled pilot can beat, found by running this same physics offline. Level 1's ideal circularization measures 604 m/s against a par of 712. The integrator has no random element, so re-running test-physics.js reproduces every figure exactly.

How it works

ΔKE = v·Δv + ½Δv²  ·  ε = ½v² − μ/r

Gravity is planet-centred and Newtonian, integrated with velocity Verlet at a fixed substep — a symplectic scheme, which is why the orbit does not spiral in from numerical drift: measured energy drift over fifty orbits is 9 × 10⁻¹⁴. The orbit drawn as a solid line is the integrated path; the dotted preview is the same integrator run forward from the burn you are planning.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Two bodies, plus at most one moon. No sun, no other planets, no J₂ oblateness, no third-body perturbations.
  • No atmosphere and no drag. A periapsis just above the surface is free here and would not be in reality.
  • Burns in stages 1 and 2 are impulsive — the whole Δv at one instant. Stage 3 removes that assumption; nothing else does.
  • Δv is the only cost. There is no mass, no specific impulse and no rocket equation, so the tank is a Δv budget rather than kilograms of propellant. That is the standard mission-planning currency, but it hides the fact that a real ship gets lighter as it burns.
  • The craft is a point mass with no orientation, so pointing the engine is free and instant.

Sources

Boundary: this is not a mission planner. It teaches one relationship — energy gained per unit Δv scales with current speed — and it deliberately strips away everything that would otherwise compete for attention. Do not use its numbers to plan anything that flies.

Live the escape test

"Escaping" is checked against the integrated state each frame: specific orbital energy ε above zero and a radius past the system edge. A tall ellipse that crosses the edge with ε still negative is refused, and the page says why.

Measureddeterministic — no seed the 661 / 1,835 m/s comparison

Both figures are the minimum prograde Δv that escapes from Level 3's orbit, found by bisection against this exact engine: 661 m/s from periapsis, 1,835 m/s from apoapsis, a ratio of 2.77. The simulated periapsis figure also matches the closed-form vesc − vp to three figures, which is the check that says the integrator and the theory agree.

Illustrative Level 5's moon

Invented, and the map says so beside it. Its orbit radius, mass parameter and size were chosen so that a useful gravity assist fits inside a class period; our own Moon is both further out and lighter relative to this setup. Its gravity is then integrated for real, including the indirect term (the moon pulls Earth too, so the planet-centred frame is not inertial).

How it works

Δε = v·Δv + ½Δv²

A burn changes only the kinetic term: the potential −μ/r is unchanged at the instant of an impulsive burn, because you have not moved. So the energy a given Δv buys is set by v, and v peaks at periapsis. The HUD's "energy gained per 1 Δv" readout is therefore numerically just your current speed — a claim the test suite verifies numerically rather than asserting.

The usual next question is where the extra energy comes from. The propellant's chemical energy is fixed; what changes is how it is divided between ship and exhaust. At high speed the engine acts over more distance per second, so more of that energy ends up in the ship and less in the plume left behind.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The burn is impulsive — instantaneous, at one speed. Real burns are not, which is exactly what stage 3 is for.
  • The system edge is a chosen radius, not a physical boundary. It stands in for "far enough that Earth no longer dominates", and each level sets its own.
  • The gravity assist is a restricted three-body problem: the moon moves on a fixed circular orbit and the craft never affects it. A real assist perturbs the assisting body, imperceptibly.
  • Steering is free. Pointing the ship costs no fuel and no time.

Live the powered arc

A finite-thrust burn is integrated with RK4 while the engine is lit, then the coast returns to velocity Verlet. The planner's burn duration is Δv divided by the engine's acceleration, and the percentage beside it is that duration against your current orbital period — both read off the live state.

Measureddeterministic — no seed775 / 919 and 731 / 1,498 m/s

On Level 6's 0.055 m/s² engine, escape costs 775 m/s with the burn centred on periapsis and 919 m/s lit at periapsis. On Level 7's 0.013 m/s² engine, six kicks of roughly 2.8 hours each cost 731 m/s where one long centred burn costs 1,498. All four are minima found against this engine offline.

Reasonedour inference from the measurement above; no specific flown mission sourced here why low-thrust stages use repeated perigee burns

The 731-against-1,498 result is the reason the technique exists: if your engine cannot deliver its Δv inside a short arc, splitting it across successive periapsis passes keeps every unit of it near maximum speed. We reason that from the measurement on this page rather than from a flown example — we did not source a specific mission's burn plan, and a presenter should present it as the principle, not as a citation.

How it works

tburn = Δv / a  ·  centre the arc on periapsis

Integrate v·Δv over the burn rather than evaluating it once. Every second of thrust is delivered at whatever speed you happen to have then, so the cheapest single burn is the one whose arc is symmetric about the speed peak. Light it at the peak and the entire second half happens on the way back out, at falling speed. When the burn is longer than roughly a third of the orbit, no placement saves it and the answer is to split it.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Steering losses are ignored. The thrust is applied along the chosen direction for the whole burn with no cosine loss from the velocity vector rotating underneath it. This is the largest simplification in the stage and it makes finite thrust look slightly cheaper than it is.
  • Thrust and acceleration are constant. No throttling, no mass loss, so no acceleration ramp as the tank empties.
  • The engine lights and cuts instantly, with no ignition transient.
  • Time‑warp is capped at 10× while thrusting, so a long burn is integrated finely enough to be honest rather than fast.

Sources

Stage by stage

Stage 1 — Burn at apoapsis (Levels 1–2)

Question

You are at the top of an ellipse and you push forward. Which part of the orbit moves?

Predict prompt

Burn forward at apoapsis. Does periapsis rise, does apoapsis rise, or do both? Three buttons, captured, echoed beside the result.

Activity

Level 1 circularizes an ellipse; Level 2 raises apoapsis into a band. The two together are the stage, because between them they show the rule in both directions.

Expected observation

The dotted preview lifts the far side while the near side barely moves. The observation cue fires at each apsis with the speed there, naming apoapsis as the minimum energy-per-Δv point.

Check-yourself answer key

Because it raises the point half an orbit away, which from apoapsis is periapsis. The two wrong options are "apoapsis is where the engine is most efficient" (the refutation item; the opposite is true and stage 2 measures it) and "a single burn reshapes the whole orbit at once".

Teaching limit

Do not let this stage become the lesson. It is vocabulary and geometry; nothing about cost is established yet, and a student who leaves thinking apoapsis is "the good place to burn" has acquired the misconception stage 2 exists to break.

Stage 2 — Escape at periapsis (Levels 3–5)

Question

Escaping Earth needs a fixed amount of energy — so does it cost the same Δv wherever you burn?

Predict prompt

Level 3 starts on an ellipse with periapsis 11,100 km and apoapsis 70,100 km above the surface. Which burn point escapes for less Δv? The flight view does not appear until this is answered.

Activity

→ Pe, 🔥 Plan burn, push Δv until the ORBIT chip flips from BOUND to ESCAPING. Then have them undo and try it from apoapsis on the same tank: that comparison is the stage. Levels 4 and 5 apply the same idea to a transfer and a powered flyby.

Expected observation

661 m/s from periapsis, 1,835 from apoapsis, a factor of 2.77 — both minima found against this exact engine. The HUD's "energy gained per 1 Δv" reads 6.09 km/s at periapsis and 1.39 at apoapsis: the same numbers, seen as they happen.

Check-yourself answer key

Item 1: because you are moving fastest there, and the energy added is v·Δv + ½Δv². The refutation option is "because you are closer to Earth" — see Misconception 1. Item 2: no — an unpowered assist borrows the moon's motion; Oberth is about when you spend fuel. See Misconception 2.

Teaching limit

2.77× belongs to this ellipse. Round the orbit out and the gap shrinks; on a circle there is no periapsis to prefer. What generalises is the direction of the effect, not its size, and the Details drawer says so in as many words.

Stage 3 — Finite thrust (Levels 6–7)

Question

Your engine needs hours to deliver its Δv. Where in the orbit should that burn happen?

Predict prompt

The full burn lasts a noticeable arc. Light it exactly at periapsis, or start before periapsis?

Activity

Levels 1–5 use impulsive burns; 6 and 7 give the engine 0.055 and 0.013 m/s². Three readouts let students compute an ignition point instead of guessing: the HUD's Time to periapsis, the planner's Burn duration, and the lead box beside → Pe, which stops the warp that many minutes short. Locked until a student has escaped in stage 2.

Expected observation

Level 6: 775 m/s centred on periapsis, 919 lit at periapsis, against an impulsive ideal of 661 — finite thrust always costs something and timing decides how much. Level 7's engine is weak enough that one long burn smears round most of the orbit for 1,498 m/s, where six kicks of roughly 2.8 hours each cost 731.

Because all of it happens after the fast point, at falling speed. The refutation option is "a longer burn wastes more fuel as heat" — see Misconception 4.

Teaching limit

Steering losses are ignored entirely, and there is no rocket equation, so the finite-thrust penalty here has the right shape and not the right size. Treat 775-against-919 as "centring matters, and roughly this much".

Stage 4 — Sandbox (outside the path)

No goal, no par, 200 units of Δv, all three engines selectable, a second fictional moon, nothing scored. Its Predict card asks for a number rather than a button — how cheaply do you think you can escape? — and its check-yourself item is the synthesis one: which single quantity decided every answer across the three stages. The answer is your speed at the instant you spend the Δv.

The physics

An impulsive burn changes speed by Δv along the velocity, so kinetic energy per unit mass goes from ½v² to ½(v+Δv)²:

Δε = v·Δv + ½Δv²

The v·Δv term dominates, and it carries your speed at the instant of the burn. Gravitational potential is unchanged at that instant — you have not moved — so the whole difference between a cheap burn and an expensive one is the v in that term. On an ellipse, speed peaks at periapsis.

The HUD's "energy gained per 1 Δv" readout is therefore numerically just your current speed — worth asking a calculus class to differentiate ½v² and see why.

Where does the extra energy come from? The propellant's chemical energy is fixed; what changes is how it divides between ship and exhaust. At high speed the engine acts over more distance per second, so more of it ends up in the ship and less in the plume.

Misconceptions to catch

Misconception 1: "The burn is better at periapsis because you are closer to the planet." Proximity is not the mechanism — speed is. Δε = v·Δv + ½Δv² contains v, not r. Being deep in the well matters only because falling there is what made the ship fast; a ship equally fast far from the planet gains just as much from the same burn. It is the wrong option on stage 2's first check item.
Misconception 2: "The Oberth effect is the same as a gravity assist." An unpowered assist (Level 5's moon) takes energy from the moon's orbital motion — no fuel, and the moon slows imperceptibly in exchange. An Oberth burn spends fuel and merely spends it at the most profitable moment. A powered flyby combines both, which is what Level 5 actually is.
Misconception 3: "Δv is like distance — it costs the same anywhere." The Δv spent is the same anywhere; the energy it buys is not. That is the whole game, and it is the third wrong option in stage 2.
Misconception 4: "A long burn costs more because the engine wastes fuel." The engine never changes within a level. What changes is the speed at which each second of thrust is delivered. It is stage 3's refutation item.
Misconception 5: "A very tall ellipse has escaped." Escape is ε > 0, not a distance. A student who crosses the system edge while still bound is told so on screen, with their actual ε quoted, because the geometry is genuinely misleading.

Discussion questions

  1. Level 3's energy plot shows a taller jump in ε for the same Δv at periapsis. Which term of Δε = v·Δv + ½Δv² explains this, and what would have to be true for the other term to matter more?
  2. The HUD says "energy gained per 1 Δv = your current speed". Differentiate ½v² and show why.
  3. Why does a prograde burn at periapsis raise the apoapsis, on the far side of the orbit?
  4. In Level 5, where does the escape energy come from when you fly close behind the moon without burning? Name the thing that slows down in exchange.
  5. A mission that wants to be captured into orbit has to lose energy. Where on the approach should it burn, and does the same argument run backwards?
  6. Could you escape with a radial-out burn? Try it, then explain from the formula why pushing "straight up" wastes fuel.

Do not quote these numbers as spaceflight figures. The model has no atmosphere, no mass loss and so no rocket equation, no steering cost, and no third-body perturbation beyond the one fictional moon. It isolates one relationship by stripping away everything that would compete with it.

Presenter controls

  • ⚙ Settings holds the three presenter controls in order — Open Presenter Notes (this page, on screen), Presentation mode (larger type plus a header Notes button) and Reset.
  • Reset restores a first load in place, with no page reload: Level 1 on the pad, an empty Δv tally, trajectory and undo stack cleared, warp back to 1×, the HUD open, all three predictions forgotten, stage 3 locked again, badges and saved name cleared. It leaves two things alone on purpose: presentation mode, since re-shrinking the projector between rooms would be hostile, and the saved class leaderboards, which hold other students' entries — use Clear scores, which asks first.
  • Keys: Space pause · B burn planner · Enter commit · Esc cancel · Z undo · X cut engine · , . warp down/up · P / A warp to Pe / Ap · H HUD · F follow camera · M briefing · R restart level · ? Guide · N Presenter Notes.
  • Undo rewinds the last burn and refunds the fuel, and is offered again after a crash. Students should iterate, not restart.
  • The four stage tabs jump between the lesson's beats; picking a level from 🗺 Levels moves the tabs with it. A #stage-3 link clamps to the furthest unlocked stage rather than routing around the gate.
