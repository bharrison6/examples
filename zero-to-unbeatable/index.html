    How to play

    Three things people call AI

    Tic-tac-toe against three different opponents. They are numbered along the top, and they are not three difficulty levels — they are three completely different ideas about where a machine's skill comes from.

    1. 1 · Rules. Eight if/else rules a person wrote down. It has played zero games and it cannot be beaten — the app searches every game that could still be played against it and finds no line where it loses. Every rule it uses is listed on the screen, and it tells you which one it just used and why. Turn rules off with First 2 / First 4 / All 8: two rules is what most people write first, and the app will show you the game where that loses.
    2. 2 · Learning. No rules at all. A list of numbers, every one of them zero, that has never played a game. Beat it easily, then press TRAIN — it plays 5,000 games against itself and against chance and freezes the result as a new era. Play the new era. Train again. Somewhere around the third to fifth burst the same exhaustive search stops finding a line where it loses.
    3. 3 · Ultimate. The same learner in a world one rule bigger. Nine small boards in a grid, and the square you play in decides which board your opponent plays in next — middle square, middle board. Sent to a board that is already finished? Play anywhere. Win a small board by three in a row inside it; win the match by winning three small boards in a row. A small board that fills with no winner counts for neither side. One sentence of extra rule, and the approach behind step 2 falls apart in two places the app goes and measures.

    Steps 1 and 2 end up in the same place by opposite routes. One got all of its skill from a person and none from experience; the other got all of it from experience and none from a person. Both are proven unbeatable by the same search.

    Show its brain (step 2) prints the value it has learned for every empty square — gold looks promising to it, red-orange looks dangerous. Era 0 is all zeros because it genuinely knows nothing. The dropdown lets you go back and play any earlier era.

    Everything happens on this device: no network, no account, nothing stored. Reloading starts over. Reopen these instructions any time with the ? button at the top.

    At a glance

    Before you start

    Presenter mode on if you are projecting. The app opens on 1 · Rules with all eight rules on, which is where you want it. To reproduce a rehearsed run, type your seed into Rehearsal seed and press Reset to Era 0. Leave the burst on 5,000. Do not train before the session, and do not refresh mid-demo.

    Step 1 — Rules, 5 minutes

    • 0:00 Hand it over: “Beat it.” They cannot. Let two or three people try and fail.
    • 1:30 Read the rule that fired under the board, then the eight-rule list beside it. “A person wrote every line of that. It has played zero games.”
    • 3:00 Switch to First 2. Now they win. Read the panel: rules 3 to 8 are greyed out, and the app shows the exact game it loses — the killing move is a fork, which is rule 3.
    • 4:00 Back to All 8 and read the proof line. Then the honest bit: for a board this small, the rules are the better engineering.

    Step 2 — Learning, 10 minutes

    • 5:00 Switch to 2 · Learning. “This one has no rules in it at all.” Let the room beat Era 0. Let them laugh at it.
    • 6:30 Show its brain — every square +0.00, seen 0 times. That is the whole mind.
    • 7:30 Press Train and say nothing for three seconds. Point at ε falling and the win rate climbing.
    • 9:00 Play Era 1. It blocks. Still beatable about one game in six — ask what it is missing.
    • 11:00 Train, play Era 2. Wins dry up, draws start. Let the frustration land; it is the lesson.
    • 13:00 Train twice more. The banner lands between the third and fifth press. Invite the best player to try.
    • 14:30 Go back to Era 0 and stomp it again. Same code, different numbers. “Nobody wrote a rule. It played twenty thousand games.”

    Step 3 — Ultimate, 5 minutes

    • 15:00 Switch to 3 · Ultimate. Read the one new rule off the caption between the panes: “the square you play decides which board it plays in next.” Play three or four moves and let them feel the line under the panes change every turn.
    • 16:30 Train once. Open the card and read the control row first: it takes a free win inside a board about four times in five. Then the row under it — a move that wins the whole match scores exactly the same to it. “It cannot see the game it is playing.”
    • 18:00 Second card: it hands you a free win at about the rate you would get by not looking, because it is not looking. Then the two hand-written opponents — it improves against the one that shares its blind spot and flatlines against the one that does not.
    • 19:00 Close on the banner: this game is solved — forced first-player win, at most 43 moves, proved in 2020 — and the app still cannot prove one thing about the opponent on the screen. A proof does not require checking every case.

    The close

    Steps 1 and 2 arrived at the same unbeatable play from opposite ends, and the same search proved both, because 255,168 games is a number you can walk. Step 3 is 1038 and cannot be walked — and it is solved anyway, by somebody who wrote down a strategy and proved it. What we have no proof about is the program. That is the ordinary situation for chess engines, self-driving cars and language models alike.

    If it goes sideways

    • Somebody beats the eight-rule bot? They did not — check the mode; it will be on First 2 or First 4. The proof line under the board says which.
    • Short of time? Do step 1 and step 2 only. Or in step 2, press train twice in a row and skip Era 2.
    • A student beats Era 3? They cannot beat a verified era, but they can beat one before the banner. Say so, then train again.
    • “It was secretly programmed.” Step 1 was — that is the point of showing it first. Then show the brain at Era 0 and run Settings → the full self-test in front of them.

    Setup (60 seconds)

    Open the file. Turn on Presenter mode if projecting. To reproduce a rehearsed run exactly, type your seed into Rehearsal seed and press Reset to Era 0. Leave the burst on 5,000 — the arc is tuned for it and takes three to five presses.

    The shape of it. Three opponents on one board, and they are not three difficulty levels. Step 1 got all of its skill from a person and none from experience. Step 2 got all of it from experience and none from a person. They end up playing identically, and the app proves both with the same search. Step 3 is where the shortcut behind step 2 stops describing the game, and where the space stops being searchable at all.

    Step 1 — rules (5 minutes)

    0:00
    Hand it to a student. "Beat it." They cannot. Let two or three people try. It answers instantly and it never slips.
    "This has played zero games too. But it is not going to lose."
    1:30
    Read the line under the board, then the list beside it. After every move it prints the rule that fired and why — "Rule 2 · Block — you had two in the top row" — and outlines the squares that set it off. The eight rules are listed in the order they are checked, and the one that fired is lit up.
    "A person sat down and wrote every line of that. There is no mystery in this one at all. You can read the whole mind of it, and it fits in eight lines."
    3:00
    Switch the ladder to "First 2". Win and block only — what almost everybody writes first. Now the room beats it, and the panel shows why: rules 3 to 8 are struck through, and the app draws the exact game it loses. The move that kills it makes two threats at once, which is rule 3, the first one switched off.
    "It did not play badly. Nobody wrote it a rule for this."
    4:00
    Back to "All 8", read the proof line, then say the honest thing. Every game playable against the full ladder was searched — 1,384 of them — and it loses none. Then: for a board this small the rules are the better engineering. Shorter, faster, finished before the learner had played a game, and checkable by reading them.
    "So why would anybody bother with the other thing?"
    That is the bridge into step 2, and it is the right question.

    Step 2 — learning (10 minutes)

    5:00
    Switch to "2 · Learning" and hand it over again. Era 0 has never played a game and has no rules in it either. It loses fast and stupidly — it will ignore a row you have almost finished. Let two or three people beat it. Let the room laugh at it.
    "This one has no rules in it at all. It does not know what winning is. It knows how to put a mark in an empty square, and that is all."
    6:30
    Press "Show its brain". Every square reads +0.00, seen 0 times.
    "That is the entire mind of your opponent. Nine numbers, all zero. No strategy, no line of code that says 'block the row'. When every number ties it flips a coin — which is exactly what you just watched."
    7:30
    Press "Train the AI". Say nothing for three seconds. Let them watch the counter blur past five thousand games. Point at the two live readings: ε falling — how often it still tries a random square — and its win rate against random play climbing. Then read the card: it now takes a free win and blocks your row.
    9:00
    Play Era 1. Two or three games. It blocks now. It is annoying. You can still beat it — about one game in six — and when somebody does, that is your line:
    "Five thousand games of experience and you still got it. So what is it missing?"
    11:00
    Train again. Play Era 2. This is the turn. Wins dry up, draws start, somebody loses and is annoyed about it. Let that land before moving on — the frustration is the lesson.
    13:00
    Train twice more. Play the newest era. Between the third and fifth press the gold banner appears. Every game is a draw now. Invite the best player in the room to try. They will draw.
    14:30
    The beat that makes it stick: go back. Choose Era 0 from the dropdown and stomp it again. Then switch to the newest era and draw again. Same program, same code, different numbers.
    "Nobody rewrote this. The only thing that changed is a list of numbers, and the only thing that changed the numbers was playing games and being told won, lost or drew."
    16:00
    Read the banner aloud, then put the two side by side. Step 1 and step 2 now play exactly the same game and neither can be beaten, and the same search proved both. One got there from a person's head in an afternoon; the other from twenty thousand games and no author. On this board the rules were the cheaper answer — say so plainly. What the rules cannot do is exist for a game nobody can write down: tic-tac-toe has five and a half thousand positions, chess and language cannot be listed at all.
    "Nobody has ever written the eight rules for chess. That is the whole reason the other thing exists."

    Discussion questions

    1. Both opponents end up unbeatable. If you had to ship one, which, and why? Does your answer change if the board becomes 4×4? If it becomes chess?
    2. Step 1 can tell you exactly why it moved; step 2 can only say "that square scored +0.87". Is the first one an explanation and the second one not? What would you need from step 2 before you would call it one?
    3. It played 5,000 games in three seconds. How many had you played before you stopped losing at this? What does that comparison tell us — and what does it hide?
    4. Why does it deliberately play a bad square during training? What breaks if it always plays the move it currently rates best?
    5. Forty per cent of its training is against an opponent playing at random. Why not train it only against itself, which is far stronger?
    6. The last thing it learns is that answering two opposite corners in a corner loses. Why would that arrive after "block the row"?
    7. Once trained, every opening square scores the same. Has it failed to learn something, or learned something?
    8. It can never lose now. Is it good at tic-tac-toe, or has it memorised tic-tac-toe? Is there a difference, and does the difference matter?

    Misconceptions to draw out

    It was secretly programmed with strategy and just pretends to start dumb.
    Meet this head on, and use step 1 to do it: that one was programmed with strategy, you showed them the eight rules, and it looks nothing like step 2 from the outside. Then show the brain at Era 0 — every value exactly zero. Then run Settings → Run the full self-test in front of them: it chi-squared tests 9,000 moves by a newborn and reports it indistinguishable from a coin flip. The code also measures that a newborn takes a free win no more often than chance. A hidden rule would show in both.
    It understands tic-tac-toe now.
    It has a number attached to each of about five thousand board pictures. It cannot say what a row is, cannot play on a 4×4 board, cannot explain one choice. Ask the room what "understands" would have to mean and whether anything on screen meets it. Best argument you can have in the last two minutes.
    More training always keeps making it visibly better.
    Watch the card across bursts. The first changes almost everything; by the third the landmark positions stop moving at all and only "positions it would now play differently" keeps falling. Improvement goes invisible, then stops — it has hit the ceiling of the game, and nothing goes past a draw.

    And if someone says it is unbeatable only because it has won a lot: it is not a win record. After every burst the app searches every game still playable against it, in both roles, following every square you could pick and every coin flip it could make — about 15,000 complete games in some 20 milliseconds — and finds no line where it loses. That is a proof, not a sample.

    What is actually running

    Tabular afterstate value learning: one number per board position meaning "how did this turn out for whoever just moved". Update is +1 for a win, 0 for a draw, otherwise the best thing the opponent can do to you, negated and discounted. Learning rate 0.8, discount 0.95, ε from 1.00 to 0.25 over 14,000 games, 60% self-play and 40% versus random play in both roles, 20% of games started from a dealt position so rare endgames get practised. No minimax, no heuristics, no opening book anywhere in the agent. Sources and the full test suite sit beside this file.

    Notes for the presenter

    • Short of time? Press train twice in a row and skip Era 2. It always lands by the fifth press.
    • A student beats Era 3? They cannot beat a verified era, but they can beat one before the banner. That is real and it is a gift — say so, then train again.
    • 500 and 2,000 exist to show the wobble in slow motion; they need many more presses. Keep 5,000 for a timed session.
    • The maths for 5,000 games finishes in well under a tenth of a second. The montage is stretched to about two and a half seconds so there is something to watch — the app admits this itself, and so should you.

    Step 3: ultimate tic-tac-toe (5 minutes)

    The third segment switches to ultimate tic-tac-toe: nine small boards in a 3×3 grid, and one extra rule — the square you play in decides which board your opponent must play in next. Sent to a board that is already finished, won or full? Play anywhere. Win a small board by three in a row inside it; win the match by winning three small boards in a row. A small board that fills with no winner counts for neither side. Run this after the banner, when the room has just watched something become perfect. One sentence of extra rule, and the method that got them there stops describing the game.

    0:00
    Switch to "3 · Ultimate" and play four or five moves. Do not explain the rule — the line under the two panes explains it on every single turn, and the gold ring shows the board you are stuck in. Let them notice that it moves because of the square they just played.
    "Same opponent. Same table. One extra sentence in the rules."
    1:30
    Train once, then read the first card — the control row first. It takes a free win inside a board about four times in five: 81% over a thousand measured matches. That row is there so the next one means something. The next one is a move that wins the whole match, and it is taken at 79% — no more often.
    "It cannot see the game it is playing. Winning three boards in a row is about where boards sit. It only ever looks inside one board."
    When the match-winning move is the only board win going, it takes it at the control rate. When another board win ties with it, it flips a coin.
    3:00
    Second card: where it sends you. It hands over an immediate win on 36% of the turns where that was a choice, against 41% for choosing blind — and 45% of those giveaways had an equally top-scoring square right beside them.
    "It is giving the game away at the rate you get by not looking, because it is not looking."
    Then the third card: two hand-written opponents, the same player apart from three clauses about the new rule. It learns against the one that shares its blind spot — 122 losses down to 54 of 200 — and flatlines against the other, 167 down to 137 and no further.
    4:00
    Close on the banner, which is the best thing in the act. Ordinary tic-tac-toe: 255,168 complete games, so steps 1 and 2 could walk every one and print a proof. Ultimate: about 3.6 × 1038 positions — counting a billion a second since the big bang leaves you 820 billion times short. And it is solved anyway. Bertholon and colleagues proved in 2020 that the first player has a forced win in at most 43 moves, second player holding out at least 29 (arXiv:2006.02353).
    "Nobody visited those positions. They wrote down a strategy and proved it. A proof does not require checking every case."
    And still nobody has proved anything about the opponent on the screen: it is not running that strategy and this app cannot search its tree. Good, measured, never proven — which is where chess engines, self-driving cars and language models all live.

    Two more questions for this act. (1) The agent scores a move by how much it improves the one board it is played in, and ignores the other eight and where the move sends its opponent. What would a memory need to be able to represent for those to matter to it? (2) The game is solved and the space cannot be searched. How can both be true at once?
