Guide — how this works

A student asks a question. You decide how much you tell it before it answers. That is the whole demo. This Guide opens itself every time the file loads — press Esc, tap outside it, or hit Got it to get out of the way. The ? in the top right brings it back at any point, and the ⚙︎ beside it holds Open Presenter Notes, Presentation mode and Reset.
  1. Pick a question along the top of the screen. These are things people really ask.
  2. On the left are three levers. Each one adds a block of text to the question: context says who is asking, success spec says what a finished answer looks like, expert keywords says which vocabulary to answer in. The box underneath shows the whole prompt as it grows, colour-coded to match.
  3. Under each lever is a line telling you what leaving it in that position will cost you, before you ask anything. Lever 2's cost is an object you can see: the deck of target cards sitting inside it, which it deals from when you haven't said what a finished answer looks like. Turn lever 2 on and watch the deck get struck out and pushed aside — you took the decision back.
  4. Press Ask. The answer types itself out. There are 64 answers baked into this file, one for every combination — nothing is generated live, so it behaves the same every time you run it.
  5. Two counters do the arguing for you: the expert-term counter under every answer, and the Scoreboard. Both are explained below.

Every control on the screen

The three leversFlip one on and its text is appended to the prompt. Flip it off and it is gone. Nothing else about the question changes, so any difference in the answer came from that lever.
Step 2 boxThe literal text being sent. If it is not in this box, the AI does not know it.
AskSends it. Skip typing jumps to the end of the animation; tapping the answer does the same.
The deck inside lever 2Somebody has to decide what a good answer looks like — how long, for whom, in what format. That decision always gets made. The only question is by whom. Lever 2 off: you haven't decided, so the AI deals itself one of the three cards shown in the lever and silently answers to it. Press Ask and it shows you the card it drew, which is the one courtesy the real thing never extends. Lever 2 on: the deck is struck out and shoved aside inside the lever, because you just took that decision off it. The deck lives inside lever 2 because it is lever 2 — it is what that lever is for.
Card it will drawIn the lever 2 panel, presenter mode only. Normally Random, which is the honest version. Pick a specific card to rehearse it. Show that card again re-plays the draw for the answer currently on screen.
Expert termsUnder every answer. It counts the specialist words the answer used, and how many of those are words the student never typed. Turn on Show me which ones and they light up in the text. With lever 3 off you will see one or two. With it on you will see fifteen or more, nearly all of them words nobody in the room supplied. That is the point of the lever.
1–10 stripAsk the class how useful the answer was and tap the number. It goes on the Scoreboard, which plots score against how many levers were pulled. After six or seven answers the curve climbs on its own and you do not have to argue for it.
Pin to compareFreezes the answer on screen in a second column so the next answer lands beside it. The fastest way to show one lever's effect. Press it again to unpin.
Presenter vs StudentFive concrete differences, listed in the coloured banner under the toolbar whenever you switch: 1. Type size — presenter is sized for a projector, student for a phone or laptop held at arm's length. 2. The guided walkthrough only appears in student mode: an eight-step tour that sets the levers for you, one at a time, and finishes with three “which lever would fix this?” questions with written feedback on every option. 3. The 1–10 score strip under each answer is presenter-only, since it is the class scoring out loud. 4. Scoreboard and Pin to compare are presenter-only tools. 5. The card picker in lever 2 is presenter-only; a student always gets a random draw, which is the honest version. Everything else — all four questions, all 64 answers, the deck, the counter — is identical in both. Hand a student a phone in student mode and they can run the whole lesson alone.
Closing slideThe line to end on, plus what the class scored.
? — Guide (top right)This sheet. It opens by itself on every load and this button brings it back — mid-demo, mid-answer, from a phone. It never goes away, at any screen width.
⚙︎ SettingsThe small menu next to it. Open Presenter Notes is the stage script; Presentation mode is the projector build of the app (big type, class scoring, compare, card picker), and switching it off puts you in student mode; Reset puts the whole demo back to a fresh load. Below those, the expert-term highlighter, a way back to this Guide, and the closing slide. On a phone the mode switch and the closing slide live here rather than on the toolbar.
Open Presenter NotesSettings → Open Presenter Notes, or press N. The printable presenter sheet itself, on screen: the three-act roleplay as stage notes — what to say, what to do with the printed deck of target cards, and the line each act lands on — plus the house rules, the running order, the discussion questions and the eight target cards. It is not a summary of the presenter sheet; the build injects that file, so the paper, the PDF and this overlay cannot disagree.
ResetSettings → Reset. Question 1, all three levers off, no answer on screen, the scoreboard cleared, the highlighter off, the card picker back to Random — exactly the state you get by reloading the file. Presentation mode is left where you set it, because it is a property of the room rather than of the demo.

The shape of the twelve minutes

0:00Play the Stranger. Walk up to a student, say the bare prompt out loud, answer it as blandly as you can.
2:00Scenario 1, all levers off. Draw the card. Score it. Put the number on the board.
4:00One lever at a time. Score each. The curve does the arguing for you.
7:00Scenario 2 with keywords on. Turn on the highlighter. Count the words nobody typed.
10:00Their turn: which lever would fix this? Then the closing line.
