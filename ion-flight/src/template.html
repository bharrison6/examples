Same charge, different masses

Both ions carry the same single positive charge. Which one reaches the detector first, and by how much?

Before you start. An ion is an electrically charged atom or molecule. A dalton (Da) is the mass unit for atoms and molecules. This instrument gives every ion a push with the same voltage, then times how long it takes to coast down a tube in vacuum — so the only thing that differs between these two ions is mass.

Predict

Which packet arrives first — 100 Da or 400 Da, both at +1?

Try

Commit to a prediction, then press Run detector and watch both packets cross the tube.

Takeaway

Flight time grows with the square root of mass, not with mass: four times the mass takes twice the time.

Same mass, different positive charges

Both ions weigh 200 Da. If one carries twice the charge, what does the same voltage do to it?

Before you start. Stage 1 held charge fixed and changed mass. Now the reverse. The charge number z counts how many elementary charges an ion carries — +1e is one, +2e is two. The voltage does work on charge, so a doubly charged ion is pushed twice as hard out of the same voltage.

Takeaway

The same voltage gives a doubly charged ion twice the energy, so it crosses the tube sooner even at identical mass.

Different values, equal mass-to-charge ratio

Ion A is 200 Da at +1 and Ion B is 400 Da at +2. What does the detector record?

Before you start. Stages 1 and 2 changed mass and charge one at a time. This stage changes both together. m/z is the conventional mass-to-charge label: divide the mass value by the charge number, so 200 at +1 and 400 at +2 both give 200. Watch what that does to two ions that are, by any other measure, different substances.

Predict

Equal m/z, different mass and charge. One peak or two?

Try

Commit, then press Run detector and look at where the two centre ticks land on the trace.

Takeaway

Flight time fixes m/z and nothing more, so one peak does not establish one kind of ion.

Demo 13 of 17 · Part four · Chemistry · Stage 4

When do two peaks count as separate?

A 1.00 m tube cannot tell 200 Da from 205 Da. Does making the tube longer fix that?

Before you start. Real ion packets do not all arrive at one exact instant, so every peak has a timing width. Sigma (σ) is the Gaussian spread of arrival times and FWHM is the full width at half maximum, which is 2.355 × σ. This stage compares the same ion pair in a 1.00 m and a 2.00 m tube under two assumed width rules — the widths are stated, not measured.

Predict

Doubling the tube doubles the gap between two arrivals. Is that enough to separate the peaks?

Try

Press Run paired comparison on the fixed-width assumption, then switch to Proportional width and run it again.

Takeaway

Whether a longer tube separates two peaks depends entirely on how the peak width behaves — the length alone does not decide it.

Symbols and units used on this page
t = L √[ m / (2qV) ]
  • t is field-free flight time in seconds, shown here in microseconds (µs). One µs is one millionth of a second.
  • L is tube length in metres (m). m is ion mass, converted from daltons (Da) to kilograms.
  • q = z·e is positive charge in coulombs. z is the dimensionless charge number; e is one elementary charge.
  • V is acceleration voltage in volts, shown in kilovolts (kV); one kV is 1,000 volts. Acceleration happens before this clock starts.
  • m/z is the conventional mass-to-charge label. For example, 400 Da with z = 2 gives m/z = 400 ÷ 2 = 200.
  • σ (sigma) is the assumed Gaussian timing spread. FWHM, the full width at half maximum, equals 2.355 σ.

In this stage, peaks count as resolved when their centre separation is at least their mean FWHM. That is a declared classroom criterion, and the timing widths are assumptions. A longer tube helps under the fixed-width assumption here; it is not a universal instrument promise. Full sources are in Details.

Guidehow to use this demo

One question across four stages: what does a flight time actually tell you about an ion?

Where this sits: an AI-built classroom tool from Part four. Its goal is to separate what a detector measures from what you may conclude, and its assumptions are one tap away in Details.

  1. Change mass. Read the two ion cards, commit to a prediction, then press Run detector and watch both packets cross the tube.
  2. Change charge. Same idea with mass held fixed and charge changed. Unlocks once stage 1 has evidence.
  3. Match the ratio. Change both at once and see what the detector can no longer separate.
  4. Peak width. Press Run paired comparison, then switch the width assumption and run it again.

Getting around

The four tabs switch stages. Details opens the formula, assumptions and sources for the current stage. Guide reopens this card; Settings holds Open Presenter Notes, Presentation mode and Reset.

Time

Twelve to fifteen minutes for the three core stages; the flight animation runs at 160 milliseconds per physical microsecond, so each run takes a few seconds. Stage 4 adds about five minutes.

Presentation mode

Scales the whole page up one notch for projecting to a room, widens the instrument, and puts a Notes button in the header.

Reset

Back to the start: stage 1, stages 2 and 3 locked again, no predictions, no evidence, the width assumption back to fixed, and every disclosure closed. The page does not reload, so nothing flashes on a projector. Presentation mode stays as you set it.

The flight time, the moving packet centres and the summed detector signal are all computed on this device from the formula below, each time you press Run. Nothing is looked up and nothing is fetched.

Scripted the ions and the instrument settings

The two ions, the 1.50 m tube and the 20 kV boost are chosen teaching values, fixed so that exactly one thing differs between the two packets. They are not a reading from a real machine.

How it works

t = L √[ m / (2qV) ]

An electric field accelerates each ion to a kinetic energy of qV. The ion then coasts through a field-free tube at whatever speed it reached, so its flight time is the tube length divided by that speed. Work the algebra and the time comes out as above: it grows with the square root of mass and falls with the square root of charge and voltage.

Because charge and voltage are the same for both ions in this stage, four times the mass gives exactly twice the time — the square root of four.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The clock times the drift only. Acceleration happens before the clock starts, and the time spent accelerating is not included. A real instrument's acceleration time is small but not zero.
  • Every ion of one kind starts identically. There is no spread in starting position or starting speed in the core stages, so each packet is a single arrival time rather than a distribution. Stage 4 is where a width is introduced, as an assumption.
  • The tube is a perfect vacuum with no field. No collisions, no stray fields, no detector response time.
  • The animation is not to scale in time. It runs at 160 milliseconds per physical microsecond — 160,000× slow motion — on one fixed scale for every stage, so that runs can be compared by eye.
  • Ions are drawn as one marker per packet. The marker is the packet's centre, not an individual ion's path.

Sources

  • Iowa State University Chemical Instrumentation Facility, Mass Spectrometry Tutorial — ions are accelerated into a field-free drift region with kinetic energy qV, and lighter ions reach the detector sooner.
  • Chemistry LibreTexts, Time-of-Flight — the same flight-time relation, and that ions "drift through this field free region at the velocity reached during acceleration" in a tube of one to two metres. This demo's 1.50 m tube sits in that range.
  • Physical constants: NIST, elementary charge (1.602176634×10−19 C, exact by definition) and NIST, atomic mass constant. These sources support the underlying physics; they do not endorse this activity.
Boundary: this is an idealized linear time-of-flight model built to make one relationship visible. It does not claim to reproduce any real instrument's timing, sensitivity or resolution, and it identifies no real substance.
What is live

The same engine, with charge as the variable

Live both arrival times

Both ions are 200 Da. The only difference the model is given is the charge number, and the 10.80 µs against 7.64 µs you see is computed from that difference alone.

How it works

KE = qV  →  v = √(2qV/m)

The voltage does work on charge, so an ion carrying two elementary charges leaves the acceleration region with twice the kinetic energy of an otherwise identical singly charged ion. Twice the energy at the same mass is √2 times the speed, which is why Ion B's time is Ion A's divided by 1.414: 10.80 ÷ √2 = 7.64.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Charge is an exact small integer. z is 1 or 2 here, with no mixed or fluctuating charge states.
  • Full acceleration for every ion. Each ion is assumed to receive the complete 20 kV of work, with no losses and no distribution of starting energies.
  • Every simplification from stage 1 still applies — drift-only clock, perfect vacuum, no starting spread.

Sources

  • Iowa State University CIF, Mass Spectrometry Tutorial — "an electric field accelerates all ions into a field-free drift region with a kinetic energy of qV, where q is the ion charge, and V is the applied voltage."
Boundary: this shows that charge changes flight time in an ideal instrument. It does not claim that charge states are easy to assign in practice, or that a real spectrum separates them this cleanly.
What is live

Two different ions, one computed arrival time

Live the coincidence

The two times are not merely close, they are equal to the last digit the model carries: this page checks them for exact equality and reports a coincident arrival on that basis, rather than on a tolerance.

How it works

Rearrange the flight-time formula and the only property of the ion that survives is the ratio m/q. Ion A is 200 Da at +1 and Ion B is 400 Da at +2, so both have m/z = 200, and an ideal clock cannot separate them. The two centre ticks on the trace land on the same time, and the summed signal shows one peak where a naive reading would expect two.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • This is the ideal case, constructed. The two ions were chosen to share an m/z exactly. In a real sample the coincidence is usually approximate, and whether two species overlap is a question about the instrument's resolution.
  • m/z is all this model uses. A real spectrometer can bring isotope patterns, fragment spectra, chromatographic separation and accurate-mass calibration to bear. None of that exists here, which is exactly why flight time alone is not enough.
  • Equal counts. Both packets are given the same 150 ion counts, so relative peak height carries no information in this activity.

Sources

  • Iowa State University CIF, Mass Spectrometry Tutorial — flight time in the drift region is set by the mass-to-charge ratio.
Boundary: the claim here is narrow and it is the important one: a single flight time constrains m/z and nothing else. This demo does not claim that real identification is impossible, only that this measurement alone cannot do it.
What is live, and what is assumed

The arithmetic is computed; the widths are stated

Live separations, widths and the verdict

For each tube length the page computes both arrival times, their separation, each peak's FWHM from the assumed sigma, and whether the separation clears the mean FWHM. The table is that computation, not a stored result.

Scriptedassumed, not measured from any instrument the two width rules

Both width rules are assumptions this activity declares. A fixed σ of 0.06 µs and a σ of 1.5% of flight time are teaching choices picked to make the two behaviours distinguishable. Neither is a measurement, and neither is claimed to describe a real spectrometer.

Scripted the resolution criterion

"Resolved when centre separation is at least the mean FWHM" is a declared classroom rule for this comparison. Real resolution and real identification use their own defined criteria.

How it works

FWHM = 2√(2 ln 2) σ ≈ 2.355 σ

Separation between the two centres is proportional to the tube length, so doubling the tube doubles it: 0.089 µs becomes 0.179 µs in both runs. What changes is the width. Under the fixed rule the width stays at 0.141 µs, so the separation-to-width ratio goes from 0.633 to 1.266 and crosses the criterion. Under the proportional rule the width doubles too, from 0.256 µs to 0.512 µs, the ratio stays at 0.350, and the pair remains overlapped. The tube length is not what decides it.

Assumptions and simplifications

  • The width rules are assumed, and the answer depends on which one you assume. That dependence is the lesson of this stage, not a limitation to be apologised for.
  • Both peaks are Gaussian and of equal area. Real peaks are often asymmetric.
  • Only the drift contributes. A real instrument's peak width also has contributions from the ion source, the detector and the electronics; none are modelled here.
  • Nothing here is a resolution specification. Clearing this page's criterion is not a claim that any instrument could distinguish 200 from 205 Da, nor that it could identify either.

Sources

  • Chemistry LibreTexts, Time-of-Flight — flight tubes of one to two metres, and ions drifting at the velocity reached during acceleration. The tube lengths compared here are in that range.
  • FWHM = 2√(2 ln 2) σ is a property of the Gaussian, not a citation: it is the constant GAUSSIAN_FWHM in this page's model, computed rather than looked up.
Boundary: this stage compares two assumptions about peak width to show that "longer tube" is not by itself an answer. It reports no real instrument's resolution, and the verdict in the table is against this page's own stated criterion only.
