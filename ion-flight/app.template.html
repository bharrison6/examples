Guided time-of-flight investigation

Time the flight. Read what arrives.

Analyze charged particles by timing their flight. In this simplified instrument, a voltage gives each ion a boost, the ions coast through a vacuum, and a detector records when each packet arrives.

An ion is an electrically charged atom or molecule.

1 · BoostVoltage adds kinetic energy before the clock starts.
2 · CoastIons cross a 1.50 metre field-free tube in vacuum.
3 · DetectArrival time becomes the measured evidence.

Optional advanced extension · When do two peaks count as separate?

Real ion packets do not all arrive at one exact instant, so each peak has a timing width. This extension compares 200 Da and 205 Da ions at +1 charge in a 1.00 m tube and a 2.00 m tube. It uses two stated, assumed width rules. Sigma (σ) is the Gaussian timing spread, and FWHM is full width at half maximum. This activity counts centers as resolved when separation is at least mean FWHM; that is a stated classroom comparison rule, not a universal resolution or identification guarantee.

Methods, symbols, and sources
t = L √[m / (2qV)]
  • t is field-free flight time in seconds, displayed here in microseconds (µs). One µs is one millionth of a second.
  • L is tube length in metres (m). m is ion mass, converted from daltons (Da) to kilograms.
  • q = z·e is positive charge in coulombs. z is the dimensionless charge number; e is one elementary charge.
  • V is acceleration voltage in volts, displayed in kilovolts (kV); one kV is 1,000 volts. Acceleration happens before this clock starts.
  • m/z is the conventional mass-to-charge label. For example, 400 Da with z = 2 has m/z = 400 ÷ 2 = 200.
  • σ (sigma) is the assumed Gaussian timing spread. FWHM, the full width at half maximum, equals 2.355σ.

For this comparison, peaks count as resolved when their center separation is at least their mean FWHM. This is a declared classroom criterion, and the timing widths are assumptions. A longer tube helps under the fixed-width assumption here; it is not a universal instrument promise.

Concept sources: Iowa State University Chemical Instrumentation Facility, Mass Spectrometry Tutorial, and Shimadzu MALDI basics. They support the underlying concepts and do not endorse this activity.

Guide

Make a prediction, then let the detector answer.

  1. Begin with Investigation 1. The colored cards are known simulated inputs: mass, positive charge, and sample name.
  2. Choose a prediction. Run stays locked until you commit to one.
  3. Select Run detector. Watch the same slow-motion scale each time, then use the arrival cues and detector trace as evidence.
  4. Read why the observation supports or contradicts your prediction, then continue from mass to charge to equal m/z.

The detector measures arrival time directly. The model uses mass and charge to simulate that time. The optional advanced extension comes after the core investigation.
