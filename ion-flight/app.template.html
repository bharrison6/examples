Which ion gets there first?

Accelerate packets through the same voltage, then let them drift. A detector clock turns their arrival times into a separation by mass-to-charge ratio, m/z.

How to use Ion Flight

Follow the packets, then read the clock.

  1. Choose a packet set. The labels give mass in Da, positive charge number z, and packet counts.
  2. Adjust flight length and acceleration voltage. Run the packets to animate a field-free drift region; the displayed microseconds are physical flight times scaled for the screen.
  3. Read the detector trace. A shared arrival means equal m/z in this ideal model, even if the two ions have different mass and charge.
  4. Switch timing-spread conventions. σ is the Gaussian standard deviation. Longer flight helps only when the absolute timing spread does not grow with arrival time.

This is a stripped-down linear TOF model, not a full ion source, reflectron, or identification workflow.

Presenter’s notes · about 12 minutes

A short classroom run of show

1. Ask for a prediction: “Does a heavier ion always arrive later?” Run the equal-m/z profile after taking votes. 2. Name the boundary: this clock separates m/z, not mass alone. 200 Da, +1 and 400 Da, +2 coincide in the ideal model. 3. Move L from short to long with fixed absolute σ. The peaks become farther apart while their absolute widths stay fixed. Then switch to fractional σ and repeat: both distance and blur grow together. 4. Use challenge 3 to make the constraint explicit. More drift length is not a universal resolution cure; the width mechanism matters. 5. Close by pointing students to the guide’s assumptions and prompts. Do not treat this simplified trace as a molecular identification result.
