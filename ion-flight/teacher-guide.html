Ion Flight

Teacher and presenter guide · Guided linear time-of-flight investigation · Murray State University

Purpose and learning target

Students analyze charged particles by timing their flight. They use three linked experiments to separate the effects of mass and positive charge, then discover why different ions with equal mass-to-charge ratio can produce one coincident detector peak.

Target claim: A time-of-flight detector measures arrival time directly. Under this ideal model, that time depends on mass-to-charge ratio, so one peak does not establish one kind of ion or identify a real molecule.

An ion is an electrically charged atom or molecule. The simplified path is: a voltage boost happens before timing, ions coast through a field-free tube in vacuum, and the detector records arrivals.

The screen uses one scale for every core run: 1.00 physical µs takes 160 ms on screen, or 160,000× slow motion. The detector trace keeps the same 0–18 µs horizontal axis.

12–15 minute core flow

  1. Orient (2 minutes). Open index.html. Use the opening how-to and identify the known simulated inputs, clock start, drift tube, and detector. Ask: “What will we know before Run, and what will the detector measure?”
  2. Investigation 1 — mass (3 minutes). Both ions are +1. Students predict, then compare 100 Da and 400 Da on the shared axis.
  3. Investigation 2 — charge (3 minutes). Both ions are 200 Da. Students predict, then compare +1 and +2.
  4. Investigation 3 — equal ratio (3 minutes). Compare 200 Da/+1 with 400 Da/+2. Let the coincident arrival motivate the conventional m/z label.
  5. Transfer (2–4 minutes). Ask whether one real detector peak proves one ion is present. Discuss what additional evidence a molecular identity would require.

Optional advanced extension: peak width

Introduce timing width before naming sigma. The extension compares 200 and 205 Da ions, both +1, in 1.00 m and 2.00 m tubes. It uses a shared 0–16 µs axis and an explicit classroom criterion: centers count as resolved when their separation is at least their mean full width at half maximum (FWHM).

Model and boundaries

qV = ½mv²   →   t = L √[m / (2qV)]
  • t is field-free flight time in seconds; L is length in metres; m is mass converted from Da to kilograms.
  • q = z·e is positive charge in coulombs; z is the charge number; V is voltage in volts.
  • The clock begins at drift-tube entry. Acceleration time is excluded.
  • The Gaussian peak uses σ as its timing spread, FWHM = 2.355σ, and area proportional to packet count.

This ideal linear TOF does not model an ion source, extraction optics, reflectron focusing, detector efficiency, calibration, fragmentation, uncertainty, or molecular identification.

Discussion prompts

  • Which values are controlled inputs, and which values are detector observations?
  • Why does the +2 ion arrive first when mass is held constant?
  • How can two different ions make one peak in the equal-ratio experiment?
  • What additional evidence would you need before identifying a real molecule?
  • In the extension, which assumed width rule lets the longer tube cross the stated criterion, and why?
